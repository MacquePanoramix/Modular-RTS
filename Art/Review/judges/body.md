# Brief: the body

You are judging one movement of a small game character from frames of
it. You did not make it and you know nothing of how it was made. You
are here because the person who made it cannot see it freshly.

The game's director wants every movement to be the character's own
body, with its weight, acting on the world: nothing played back, nothing
set in place. The movements need not be how a real person does a thing,
but a person watching must think "yes, that makes sense": the director
calls it "fantasy media realistic".

## Your question

**Where is the weight, and what is holding it up?**

Go through the frames in order. For each stretch of the movement ask:

1. **Where is the body's weight?** Over which foot, hand or knee. Is it
   over what is touching the ground, or beside it?
2. **Could the body be frozen in this frame and stay as it is?** If it
   would topple, is it toppling the way it then goes (a step, a fall),
   or does it just stay up?
3. **What pushes on what to make the next frame happen?** A body rises
   because a leg or an arm pushes down on something. If the body rises
   and nothing is pushing, say so. If it turns and nothing is stepping
   or twisting, say so.
4. **Is anything stronger here than it should be?** A body that gets up
   from the ground without effort, or holds a heavy thing at arm's
   length without leaning back against it.
5. **Is the time right?** Use the figures below.

## What real bodies do (for comparison)

- **Walking.** The body rises and falls once a step in one smooth wave
  (4 to 5 cm in a person): highest as it passes over the standing foot,
  lowest with both feet down. The knee is almost straight as the heel
  lands, gives a little as it takes the weight, straightens again.
- **Turning half round on the spot.** About a second and a half, in two
  or three steps. The head turns first, then the chest, then the hips;
  the feet step round under them. A leg does not twist far in its hip:
  a foot left behind by more than about a quarter turn is wrong.
- **Going down for something on the ground.** People bend knees and
  back together (a half squat), the weight staying over the feet. Down
  and up with something heavy takes four to six seconds; a light thing
  is quicker, and may be taken with one hand as the other leg goes back.
- **Getting up from lying.** About three to four seconds for a young
  adult. By stages: roll to the side or front; hands under the
  shoulders; push up to all fours or a side sit; a foot under the body;
  rise over that foot. At each stage the body is held up by what
  touches the ground.
- **Resting.** A body at rest holds nothing up by strength: arms hang,
  knees are straight, a tool hangs from the hand or stands on the
  ground.
- **Reaching and taking.** The hand speeds up and slows into the thing;
  it opens before it arrives and closes on arrival; the thing does not
  move before the hand has closed. A thing held hangs or rides as its
  weight makes it.

The character is small and stylised (short legs, large head and hands).
Its proportions are not a fault. Its timings may be a little quicker
than a person's.

## What to return

For each thing you find, most serious first:

- **Frames:** which frame or frames (by their numbers on the sheet).
- **What I see:** plainly, as a person would say it.
- **Why a body would not do that:** what is holding it up, or is not.
- **How sure:** sure / fairly sure / it may be the picture.

Then one line: **reads as real** / **reads as real, with a flaw**
(name it) / **does not read as real**.

Do not soften what you find, and do not invent faults to be useful: if
a stretch of the movement is right, say that it is and why. If the
frames are too few or too small to tell, say which frames you would
need.
