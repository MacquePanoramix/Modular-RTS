# Getting up from the floor: body-side and strategy-side levers for a small, big-headed, short-limbed body

Scope note: the character is a ~40 kg, 13-part physics body with a large heavy head, short arms and short legs, torque-capped joint motors and no external help. Its current route is prone -> hands and knees -> feet forward -> "bear" (hands and feet, hips high) -> hands leave the floor -> roll up over the feet. It nearly always falls at the hand-release (tips back onto the heels, or forward). Half-kneel failed completely. Source reading depth is marked: "(full text)", "(abstract only)" or "(search snippet only)". Where a primary source could not be opened, that is said.

## 1. How people, especially toddlers, get up from the floor (VanSant components, developmental strategies, age trends)

### Takeaway
VanSant's scheme splits rising into three body regions (arms, axial/trunk, legs), each with an ordered set of patterns running from asymmetrical (roll to prone, push with hands, kneel or "jump to squat") to symmetrical (sit up straight forward, reach rather than push, rise from a symmetrical squat). Toddlers (15–47 months) mostly use the early, asymmetrical, hands-on-floor patterns; clinicians see toddlers rise first through a bear stance or a squat, and only later through a half-kneel once balance and strength improve. Even healthy adults are highly variable, and only a quarter use the fully symmetrical pattern.

### Cited Findings
- VanSant (1988): 32 young adults, each rising from supine 10 times, videotaped. Categories were formed for upper extremities, axial region and lower extremities. Only 25% of subjects showed a common combination: symmetrical limbs and trunk, forward flexion from supine, through sitting to squatting, then standing. VanSant ordered the categories in each region and proposed the order as a developmental sequence (abstract only). — [VanSant 1988, Phys Ther 68:185](https://academic.oup.com/ptj/article-lookup/doi/10.1093/ptj/68.2.185)
- VanSant's category steps as tabulated by Kuwabara et al. (2013), split into asymmetrical and symmetrical groups (the fetch tool warned the table layout was hard to parse, so the grouping may not be exact):
  - Upper extremity: 1 push-and-reach to bilateral push; 2 asymmetrical push; 3 symmetrical push; 4 symmetrical reach (no push).
  - Axial: 1 full rotation, abdomen down; 2 full rotation, abdomen up; 3 partial rotation; 4 forward with rotation; 5 symmetrical.
  - Lower extremity: 1 jump to squat; 2 half-kneel; 3 asymmetrical wide-based squat; 4 symmetrical narrow-based squat.
  — [Kuwabara et al. 2013, Advances in Physical Education](https://file.scirp.org/Html/31326.html) (full text)
- Another scoring use of VanSant's sequences scores upper extremity out of 4, axial out of 4, and lower extremity out of 5. So the lower-extremity sequence has 5 levels there, versus 4 in Kuwabara's table. — [Duncan et al. 2017, Sports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5968950) (full text). This is an unresolved discrepancy (see Gaps).
- Marsala & VanSant (1998): 60 children aged 15–47 months, each videotaped for at least 6 rising trials. Patterns were coded per region and compared across 10-month age bands. Two previously undescribed lower-extremity patterns were found. The youngest toddlers had the highest incidence of the patterns predicted to come first in development. Upper-extremity and axial patterns predicted to be earliest predominated in the youngest group (abstract only; the percentages are behind a paywall). — [Marsala & VanSant 1998, Phys Ther 78:149](https://academic.oup.com/ptj/article-lookup/doi/10.1093/ptj/78.2.149)
- A secondary source summarising Marsala & VanSant says the age bands were 15–25, 26–36 and 37–47 months. It reports "pike" and "pike-jump to squat" leg patterns in a small percentage of trials in all three groups, and the asymmetrical wide-based squat in a few trials, mainly in the two older groups (search snippet only; the page is bot-protected). — [comum.rcaap.pt review](https://comum.rcaap.pt/bitstreams/c5f5bdba-05e9-40c3-af2a-8674a0bb232d/download)
- Kuwabara et al. (2013), 68 typically developing children aged 3 y 5 mo to 6 y 5 mo:
  - Group sizes (asymmetrical / symmetrical): upper extremity 56 / 12; axial 19 / 49; lower extremity 21 / 47.
  - Children using the asymmetrical patterns were younger. Mean age, asymmetrical vs symmetrical: upper extremity 4 y 9 mo vs 5 y 8 mo; lower extremity 4 y 6 mo vs 5 y 1 mo.
  - Children with symmetrical upper-extremity patterns were stronger and steadier: grip 6.9 vs 4.4 kg; trunk flexor strength 65.2 vs 41.1 N; one-leg standing 24.4 vs 14.2 s.
  - The authors say younger children tend to use asymmetrical patterns, citing VanSant and Marsala & VanSant.
  — [Kuwabara et al. 2013](https://file.scirp.org/Html/31326.html) (full text)
- In 5–9-year-olds, each extra year of age raised the VanSant-based process score by just over 1 point. BMI was not a significant covariate of pattern score. — [Duncan et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5968950)
- In 30 adults (22–40 y) rising at three speeds, 87% changed at least one category with speed. The most common pattern was an asymmetrical squat, with one hand leaving the floor before the other, and a trunk that started symmetrically and then rotated slightly to one side (abstract only). — [Pacific University thesis](https://commons.pacificu.edu/works/publication-dissertation/8evd5-4x010)
- Clinical teaching material (paediatric PT clinic, not peer-reviewed) says toddlers at first come to standing through a bear stance or a squat. They move to a half-kneel as balance, strength and control improve. It also recommends support surfaces lower than the shoulder so that the child pushes rather than pulls into standing. — [Starfish Therapies, "Floor to stand"](https://starfishtherapies.com/2020/07/floor-to-stand)
- An NHS leaflet describes the typical sequence for pulling to stand at furniture: pull to the knees first, then push up into standing. — [Leeds Community Healthcare NHS leaflet](https://leedscommunityhealthcare.nhs.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/1783_Pulling-to-stand-developing-movement-skills-in-your-child-with-developmental-delay.pdf)
- WHO Motor Development Study, 816 children in 5 countries; 1st–99th percentile windows: "standing with assistance" 4.8–11.4 months, "standing alone" 6.9–16.9 months (search snippet of the publication). — [WHO MGRS windows of achievement (RTI listing)](https://www.rti.org/publication/motor-development-study-windows-achievement-six-gross-motor-development-milestones)
- Healthy control children (mean age 7) in a Gowers study usually rose from upright kneeling: knees to about 90°, hips to neutral, one hip flexed forward with the foot planted, then knees and hips fully extended. Torso flexion stayed under ~30°, the base was narrow, and the rise took ~1 s. — [Chang & Mubarak 2012, Clin Orthop Relat Res](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3369091/) (full text)

### Inferences
- The character's current route (prone -> all fours -> feet brought forward -> hips high -> stand) belongs to the earliest-developing lower-extremity family: "jump to squat" and the "pike" / "pike-jump to squat" patterns that Marsala & VanSant added for toddlers. "Pike" very probably means a hands-and-feet, hips-high posture, which is what the project calls the bear. That reading is not confirmed, because the 1998 full text could not be read. So the route matches what real toddlers with toddler proportions do.
- Half-kneel comes later in development, together with better balance and strength (clinical source above; Kuwabara's strength and balance differences). A big-headed, short-limbed body failing the half-kneel therefore fits the developmental picture. It is not an anomaly to "fix" by forcing the adult or older-adult favourite.
- Even healthy adults release the hands asymmetrically: one hand leaves first, and the trunk rotates slightly. A staggered hand release (three-point support for a moment) is a natural human feature, not a compromise.

### Gaps
- The Marsala & VanSant (1998) per-age percentages, the exact definitions of the two new toddler leg patterns, and how toddlers time hand release are all behind the Phys Ther paywall. I could not read them. VanSant (1990, children 4–7 y) could not be retrieved at all (OUP 404; PubMed blocked).
- I found no peer-reviewed study that describes whether toddlers rising from a deep squat put their hands on their knees or on the floor, or that measures toddler momentum use in rising. Only clinical and parent material was found.
- The exact level list of VanSant's lower-extremity component is unresolved: 4 levels in Kuwabara, 5 in Duncan's scoring. A "kneel" level, as I recall it from the literature, could not be confirmed from a readable source.

## 2. How the moment the hands leave the floor is managed (humans and small humanoid robots)

### Takeaway
No human study found measures the hand-release moment directly. But three lines of evidence agree. Weak humans keep hand support through the transfer by moving the hands from the floor to the thighs (Gowers). Normal adults lift one hand before the other. Small robots whose arms are too short, or whose ankle and knee ranges are too limited, to bring the centre of mass (COM) over the feet statically must use a short dynamic phase: a forward swing that tips the body over the edge of the feet. That phase is reliable only when the static phases first bring the COM as close as possible to the foot support area.

### Cited Findings
- The robot paper, Stückler, Schwenk & Behnke ("Getting Back on Two Feet", KidSize humanoid "Jupp", 60 cm, 2.3 kg):
  - Getting up fails statically because the COM projection is outside the convex hull of the feet. Extra supports (knees, elbows, hands, backside) are needed to bring it back. Humans combine statically stable phases with dynamic phases to overcome limits of range and torque.
  - From supine (sit-up, then a bridge on the hands): the arms were not long enough to support the body while hips and spine shifted the COM over the feet, because of limited ankle, knee and spine ranges. So the body is swung forward while the arms lose contact. It tips over the trailing (heel) edges of the feet, and the COM projection moves from behind the heels to the front until the whole soles are down. The arms are swung forward, and the spine flexed, to accelerate the trunk forward.
  - From prone: the legs cannot fold fully, so the arms must lift a longer lever. The robot pushes until it tips over the leading edges of the feet.
  - Control window: too small a hip-swing amplitude and the motion stops before the COM is over the feet (falls back). Too large and it overshoots (falls forward over the toes). The same holds for the arm push from prone.
  - Results: 100% success in 100 lab tests. The amplitude could vary widely (h = 0.21 ± 0.025, e = 0.16 ± 0.06) because the dynamic phase was kept short by moving the COM as close as possible to the foot polygon in the preceding static phases. It also worked on hard and slippery floors and with one arm's joints relaxed.
  - The paper cites VanSant (1988) as its human inspiration.
  — [Stückler, Schwenk & Behnke, IAS-9](https://www.ais.uni-bonn.de/behnke/papers/IAS9.pdf) (full text)
- In the same paper's review: the Robotis Cycloid II (41.5 cm, 2.4 kg) could stand up statically from prone and supine thanks to powerful actuators and disproportionately long arms. HRP-2P (154 cm, 58 kg) needed a lightweight backpack, strong arms and wide joint ranges. Its rise from prone needs a short dynamic kneeling-to-crouching transition, because its knees and soles cannot touch the floor at the same time. — [Stückler, Schwenk & Behnke](https://www.ais.uni-bonn.de/behnke/papers/IAS9.pdf); [AIST HRP-2 prototype press release 2002](https://www.aist.go.jp/aist_e/list/latest_research/2002/20020919/20020919.html)
- Adults' most common rise has one hand leaving the floor earlier than the other (abstract only). — [Pacific University thesis](https://commons.pacificu.edu/works/publication-dissertation/8evd5-4x010)
- Older adults' transitions to standing include rising from all fours and rising from one knee forward, with single- or double-hand pushes. Hand-release timing was not quantified. — [Burton et al. 2023, IJERPH](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9958992/) (full text)
- Healthy and frail older adults' intermediate positions show a preference for keeping both upper- and lower-extremity contact with the floor (search snippet only). — [Ulbrich et al. 2000, JAGS](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/2027.42/111270/1/j.1532-5415.2000.tb03874.x.pdf)
- Gowers' manoeuvre (detail in section 3): with weak hip extensors, the hands do not leave the support all at once. They move from the floor to the thighs and "climb" until the hips are extended. — [Chang & Mubarak 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3369091/)

### Inferences
- The character's failure (tipping back onto the heels or forward as the hands leave) is the same under-/overshoot of a dynamic tip-over phase that the KidSize robot faced. The lever the robot paper found is to shorten the dynamic phase: get the COM as near as possible to the foot area before the hands go. The robot then had a wide, forgiving parameter window.
- For this body, levers that bring the COM closer to the feet before release include:
  - feet placed further forward under the hips;
  - deeper ankle dorsiflexion and knee flexion, so the shank can incline forward over the foot;
  - the trunk and big head kept low and forward;
  - and, after release, the arms swung or reached forward as a counter-mass.
- Humans offer two intermediate supports the robot did not use: one hand released before the other, and a hand moved from the floor to the knee or thigh. With short arms, the knee is within reach in a deep squat. A push on one's own thigh is an internal force produced by the body's own shoulder and elbow torques, so it does not break the "only its own muscles" rule.

### Gaps
- I found no human biomechanics paper that measures COM position relative to the feet, or ground reaction forces, at the instant the hands leave the floor during floor rising (Burton 2023 says it did not assess this).
- The Hirukawa et al. (2005) IJRR paper on HRP-2P's lie-down and get-up sequence, and Kanehiro et al. (2003/2007), were not read beyond bibliographic snippets.

## 3. Gowers' manoeuvre: how weak bodies get up, and why it works

### Takeaway
Gowers' manoeuvre is a graded family of compensations. They keep the body's weight line where weak hip and knee extensors need to make the least torque. The patient turns prone, uses a wide base, uses toe-standing (equinus) so the force line stays in front of the knee, keeps the trunk flexed, and walks the hands from the floor up the thighs so the arms do the hip-extension work. The earliest, mildest form is just one hand pushing on a thigh or the floor.

### Cited Findings
- Chang & Mubarak (2012) filmed 33 patients (26 M / 7 F, mean age 8, range 3–19; 41 videos) and 3 healthy controls:

| Grade | Patients (videos) | Mean age (y) | Mean rise time | Lost ambulation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | 3 | 7.3 | ~1 s | 0 |
| Mild | 11 (13) | 7.8 | 3.1 s (1–8) | 0 |
| Moderate | 15 (20) | 6.9 | 8.1 s (3–19) | 8/15 |
| Severe | 8 (8) | 8.8 | ~21 s (6–50) | 6/8 |

  — [Chang & Mubarak 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3369091/) (full text)
- Grades in that study: mild = a prolonged or strained rise, one hand on the thigh or floor, or a crawl position with independent hip extension; moderate = a prone crawl position with one or two hands on the thigh; severe = several hand moves up the thigh, an outside aid, or unable to rise. — [Chang & Mubarak 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3369091/)
- Mild-stage compensations and their stated effects:
  - Exaggerated torso flexion (near or beyond 90°) lowers the hip-extension moment.
  - A wide base, with the forward foot planted further ahead, improves balance.
  - Equinus (toe-standing) keeps the force line in front of the knee, making up for weak knee extensors.
  — [Chang & Mubarak 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3369091/)
- Moderate-stage compensations:
  - Children almost always turn prone first; the prone crawl position lowers the force needed for knee extension.
  - The hands walk back toward the feet to shift the centre of gravity over the extended legs.
  - The knees are kept extended, which the authors say maximises the knee-extension moment.
  - Arm abduction and extension help finish hip extension.
  - Wide hip abduction widens the base, at some cost to balance.
  - Anterior pelvic tilt and lumbar hyperlordosis move the centre of gravity backward to "lock" the anterior hip capsule, making up for a weak gluteus maximus.
  — [Chang & Mubarak 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3369091/)
- Severe stage: shorter hand travel; feet further apart; more equinus, which helps the knee-extension moment. Excessive equinus or flexion contractures make rising impossible without a chair or wall. — [Chang & Mubarak 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3369091/)
- Mubarak's teaching video shows wide abduction with internal rotation, which uses the iliotibial band as a knee extensor, plus arm push-off and lordosis (search snippet of the video page). — [Mubarak, "Gowers sign: a spectrum" (Global HELP)](https://global-help.org/videos/gowers-sign-a-spectrum-scott-j-mubarak-md/)
- Gowers' original 1879 description: one hand is placed on the knee and pushes while the other pushes on the ground, enough for the hip extensors to bring the trunk upright. — [LITFL, Gowers sign](https://litfl.com/gowers-sign/)

### Inferences
- For a body near its torque limits, the Gowers family gives three concrete strategy levers:
  1. Do not release both hands from the floor at once. Move one hand to the near thigh or knee first; the mild form is a single hand push.
  2. Keep the trunk deeply flexed until the COM is over the feet, then extend the hips with help from the arms.
  3. Use a wide stance for lateral stability.
- The equinus point matters for the "tips back onto its heels" failure. Being up on the forefoot keeps the ground reaction force ahead of the knee. Weak knee extensors then work less, and the COM is necessarily over the forefoot rather than behind the heel. The cost is a small support area (see section 6 on feet).
- Gowers is the strategy of a weak human with normal proportions. The character also has short arms, so the "walk up the thighs" phase is short; but its thighs are also short, so the hands may reach the knees from a deep squat. This is worth checking in the sim.

### Gaps
- Chang & Mubarak report no joint moments or ground reaction forces. The mechanism is argued qualitatively. I found no quantitative inverse-dynamics study of the Gowers manoeuvre.

## 4. Body proportions: head mass, COM height, limb lengths, and how they relate to strategy

### Takeaway
Toddlers really are top-heavy. Child crash-dummy design data (built from child anthropometry) put the head at ~25% of body mass at 12 months, ~24% at 18 months and ~21% at 3 years, against ~6–8% in adults. Sitting height is ~62–64% of stature in 1–1.5-year-olds versus ~52% in adults, so the legs are short. The head's share of mass falls and the thighs' share rises with age.

- Toddlers 1.5–3 y (photogrammetry, Jensen's elliptical model): the head's share of body mass falls with age while the thigh's rises. - Toddlers 15–36 months:
  - Segment density is lower than adults' because of more fat. Toddlers have more muscle relative to fat in the legs than newborns. Growth is cephalocaudal (the thorax grows fastest among axial segments) and distal-to-proximal.
  - Lengths estimated from the paper's regressions at the sample mean (~0.88 m, ~12.4 kg), computed by the fetch tool, so treat with caution: head ≈ 23% of stature, thigh ≈ 17%, leg (shank) ≈ 18%, upper arm ≈ 16%, forearm ≈ 14%.
  — [Toddler morphometry and segment inertial parameters (PMC2667919)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2667919)
- Infants in their first three months of independent walking (28–55 weeks old): head/neck mass about 2.16–2.43 kg across sessions. Only the head/neck, upper-trunk and lower-trunk masses changed significantly. The authors suggest the lower trunk, controlled last, may limit motor development. — [Changes in infant segment inertias during the first three months of independent walking, Dynamic Medicine 2005](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1282585/)
- Jensen (1986), boys 4–15 y: head mass proportion falls with age, balanced by rises in the thigh, shank, foot and upper arm (abstract only, no numbers). — [Europe PMC records of Jensen RK papers](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=AUTH:%22Jensen%20RK%22%20AND%20(TITLE:segment%20OR%20TITLE:infancy%20OR%20TITLE:children)&resultType=core&format=json&pageSize=15)
- Swearingen et al. (1969, FAA), ~135 infants aged 2–36 months: the centre of gravity of a 2-month-old was 6.0 in above the crotch, versus 5.9 in for a full-grown adult. In absolute terms they are almost the same, so it sits far higher relative to the infant's leg length (abstract only; the measurement may have been supine). — [Swearingen et al. 1969, DOT/FAA/AM-69/22](https://rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/20918)

### Inferences
- If the character's head is near toddler proportions (~20–25% of mass, versus ~6–8% for an adult), its whole-body COM sits high and forward on the trunk. In a bear stance, more weight then rests on the hands. This is consistent with the observation that its half-kneel kept the weight near the hands.
- A rough check: the 2-month-old's COM is ~15 cm above the crotch with ~58 cm stature (WHO-median-like length; my assumption). That is ~26% of stature above the crotch, versus ~8.5% for an adult.
- The useful comparison for tuning is the character's head mass fraction against the table above. The closer it is to 21–25%, the more the toddler strategies apply: symmetrical, hands-and-feet ("pike" / jump to squat), wide base, hands kept on the support long. Lowering the head fraction toward the 3–6-year range (~17–21%) is a body-side lever that should make the hands-free last step easier, if the look allows it.
- Short legs (sitting height ~62–64% of stature at 1–1.5 y) cut both ways. The hips are close to the ground, so the vertical lift needed is small. But the feet sit close under a top-heavy trunk, and the thigh and shank lever arms are short.

### Gaps
- I found no measured whole-body COM height as a fraction of stature for 1–4-year-olds in standing (Palmer 1944 was not retrievable; the Swearingen report's stature-relative data were not readable).
- No peer-reviewed toddler head-mass percentages were directly readable. Dummy design values are engineering targets derived from anthropometry and are not measurements of individuals.
- Arm length relative to stature for toddlers: only the uncertain regression-derived estimate above. No direct study found.

## 5. Squat-to-stand and sit-to-stand mechanics: COM over the feet, momentum, torques, joint ranges

### Takeaway
Rising needs a minimum combined hip + knee extensor moment. In slow (quasi-static) sit-to-stand this is about 1.5 N·m/kg per leg for adults, and trunk lean trades knee demand against hip demand. Bringing the trunk forward so the COM sits over or ahead of the knee before lift-off cuts knee demand by more than half. Floor rising uses roughly 120–130° of knee flexion, ~90–97° of hip flexion and ~30° of ankle dorsiflexion in older adults. A flat-footed deep squat needs more.

### Cited Findings
- Yoshioka et al. (2007), more than 5,000,000 computed sit-to-stand movements (0.40 m chair, no arms, 5 young men):
  - Per-leg peak moments ranged: hip 0.24–1.92 N·m/kg, knee 0.51–1.97 N·m/kg, ankle −0.11 to 1.32 N·m/kg.
  - Minimising hip moment (0.24) raised knee moment to 1.28 N·m/kg, with the trunk fairly upright.
  - Minimising knee moment (0.51) raised hip moment to 0.98 N·m/kg, with the trunk tilted forward at the start so the centre of gravity stayed over the knee.
  - The sum of peak hip + knee moments stayed near 1.53 N·m/kg whatever was minimised, and had to exceed that for a successful rise. A sum of ≤1.5 N·m/kg may signal a need for strengthening.
  - The ankle moment could be near zero; it is not strictly required.
  - The optimal patterns were slow (4–11 s).
  — [Yoshioka et al. 2007, BioMedical Engineering OnLine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1929086/) (full text)
- A later simulation of 718 movement variations found a hip + knee sum of 1.51 N·m/kg (search snippet). — [BMC Biomed Eng Online 2018, Table 2](https://biomedical-engineering-online.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12938-018-0610-5/tables/2)
- An experimental and simulation study found the momentum-transfer strategy was preferred normally. Under extra load without a strategy change, knee extension moments rose; changing strategy shifted effort to the hips and plantarflexors. Knee-extensor capacity limits sit-to-stand when muscles are weak (search snippet only). — [Maastricht University, "Muscles limiting the sit-to-stand movement"](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/muscles-limiting-the-sit-to-stand-movement-an-experimental-simula/)
- Floor-to-stand in 34 community-dwelling older adults: in five-times sit-to-stand the joint demands were knee extension torque 1.10–1.31 N·m/kg, hip extension 1.13–1.20 N·m/kg and ankle plantarflexion 0.49–0.52 N·m/kg, by strategy group, with no significant group differences. In an instructed half-kneel floor-to-stand without hands, ankle and hip demands were generally greater than in five-times sit-to-stand, and knee demands smaller. — [Stutzenberger & Whited 2025, Geriatrics](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12452367/) (full text)
- Peak joint angles during self-selected floor rising in 20 older adults (by strategy): hip flexion 89–97°; knee flexion 118–129°; ankle dorsiflexion 29.8–31.5°; ankle plantarflexion 27–33°. The sit-up strategy needed the most hip and knee flexion. — [Burton et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9958992/) (full text)
- Roll-over risers used on average 25.2° more knee flexion than quadruped risers (p < 0.001). — [Stutzenberger & Whited 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12452367/)
- Hemmerich et al. (2006), 30 Indian adults (mean 48 y): heels-up squatting needed a mean maximum knee flexion of 157 ± 6°. Heels-flat squatting reached a mean maximum hip flexion of 95 ± 27°. Cross-legged sitting needed 58 ± 14° of ankle flexion range (abstract only; the heels-flat ankle dorsiflexion value is in the paywalled full text, which I could not open). — [Hemmerich et al. 2006, J Orthop Res 24:770 (Europe PMC record)](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=Hemmerich%20squatting%20kneeling%20range%20of%20motion%20activities%20daily%20living&resultType=core&format=json&pageSize=5)
- In 101 adults, squat depth was predicted by ankle dorsiflexion with the knee flexed and by hip flexion range in men, and by dorsiflexion with the knee extended and by dorsiflexor strength in women (search snippet). — [Kim et al. 2015, J Hum Kinet](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4415844/)
- Shifting the centre of pressure forward during squats, from 44.4% to 53.5% of foot length measured from the heel, raised the ankle extensor moment ~30% (0.302 -> 0.432 N·m/kg/m). It lowered the knee extensor moment ~6% (0.872 -> 0.818 N·m/kg/m) and left the hip unchanged. — [JSSM 2022, anterior vs posterior COP squats](https://www.jssm.org/jssm-21-341.xml-Fulltext) (full text)
- Ankle range in children:
  - Newborns have the greatest passive dorsiflexion, about 60°, falling to about 20° in adulthood (secondary review, search snippet only; page bot-blocked). — [Uni Stuttgart review PDF](https://elib.uni-stuttgart.de/bitstream/11682/14809/1/10.1177_18632521241234768.pdf)
  - Among 337 elementary-school children, 11.9% could not squat with heels down. That group had reduced lower-limb range, and ankle dorsiflexion range plus BMI predicted squatting (abstract). — [J-STAGE, Jpn J Phys Fitness Sports Med 70(3):193](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jspfsm/70/3/70_193/_article/)
  - Younger children (7–12 y) showed greater ankle dorsiflexion in a standard squat (search snippet). — [UDESC thesis PDF](https://sistemabu.udesc.br/pergamumweb/vinculos/00006c/00006cb8.pdf)

### Inferences
- Scaling to the character, quasi-static and with my assumptions: moments in N·m/kg scale with body height. Yoshioka's 1.53 N·m/kg for a 1.74 m body is ≈ 0.88 N·m per (kg·m). For a 40 kg body of height H metres, the per-leg peak hip + knee sum needed for a chair-height sit-to-stand is ≈ 0.88 × 40 × H N·m: about 35 N·m per leg at H = 1.0 m, or 42 N·m at H = 1.2 m.
- A full squat from the floor is deeper than a 0.40 m chair, so demand will be higher; no direct number was found. If the character's leg motor caps sit near this, it will fail exactly where the momentum-transfer phase is (hands leaving).
- The knee-sparing pattern is the trunk-forward one (COM held over or ahead of the knee). For a heavy-headed body this is naturally available: the head is a large forward counter-mass. The cost moves to the hips, so hip-extensor capacity becomes the limit.
- Tipping back onto the heels at release means the COM was behind the heel edge when hand support vanished. Levers, in rough order of body-side cost:
  - more ankle dorsiflexion range (toddler-like 40–60° rather than adult ~20–30°);
  - feet set further forward under the hips;
  - a deeper knee bend so the shank inclines forward;
  - a forward reach or swing of the arms at release;
  - a slight heel lift (equinus), so the support is the forefoot and the force line stays in front of the knee.
- Tipping forward means overshoot: too much forward momentum, or the push lasting too long. The KidSize-robot lesson is to bring the COM closer to the feet before release, so the dynamic phase can be gentle.

### Gaps
- I found no study giving minimum joint torques for rising from a full floor squat (as opposed to a chair) in N·m/kg, nor any for children.
- No toddler-specific norms for ankle dorsiflexion were readable. The Thai study of 1–7-year-olds (Siritaratiwat 2006) exists, but its PDF did not load.
- The heels-flat squat ankle-dorsiflexion value from Hemmerich (2006) could not be verified (paywall). I deliberately did not use the commonly quoted ~38° figure.

## 6. Feet: support length, heel-flat versus heel-raised squats, friction

### Takeaway
The feet are the only support once the hands lift, so foot length (especially behind the ankle, against backward tipping) and how the COM is placed along the foot decide the margin. Heel-flat squats give a larger support base. Heel-raised squats concentrate support on the forefoot but keep the force line ahead of the knee, which weak children with Gowers' sign use. Raised heels in adults reduce COP sway and trunk lean.

### Cited Findings
- In squats, a COP held at 53.5% vs 44.4% of foot length from the heel changed the ankle extensor moment by ~30% and the knee moment by ~6%. — [JSSM 2022](https://www.jssm.org/jssm-21-341.xml-Fulltext)
- A network meta-analysis of 14 studies: elevated heels reduced COP deviation and trunk lean in most included studies, with ~25 mm heels suggested as the preferred option for anteroposterior squat stability (search snippet). — [MDPI Applied Sciences 2025, 15(5):2471](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/15/5/2471)
- Equinus in Gowers' patients keeps the force line in front of the knee to make up for weak knee extensors. Excessive equinus can make rising impossible. — [Chang & Mubarak 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3369091/)
- Foot length grows fastest up to age 3 (~5.3 cm of growth by then in a 0–15-year sample; search snippet). — [Children 2022, Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9139892/table/children-09-00750-t001)
- The KidSize robot (carbon-composite flat feet) stood up reliably even on hard, slippery floors (stone, hardwood) as well as carpet. — [Stückler, Schwenk & Behnke](https://www.ais.uni-bonn.de/behnke/papers/IAS9.pdf)

### Inferences
- For backward tipping at hand release, what matters is the heel length behind the ankle (the trailing edge) and how far forward of that edge the COM sits. A stylised miner with boots could legitimately get a longer heel or a broader sole, a body-side lever that does not change the strategy.
- A broader sole and a wide stance (Gowers) help lateral stability during the asymmetrical, one-hand-first release.
- Friction mattered little for the robot. But a sliding foot in a deep squat removes the forward lean the body depends on, so check the boot friction in the sim if the feet creep backward at release.

### Gaps
- I found no direct foot-length-to-stature ratio for 1–3-year-olds, and no study comparing heel-flat and heel-raised squat-to-stand success in children. The heel-elevation literature is in adults, and partly about loaded squats.

## 7. Strength-to-weight, arm help, and rising under constraints (no arms, older adults, strategies other than half-kneel)

### Takeaway
Weaker or frailer bodies choose strategies with more support points and longer support: quadruped push-ups, roll-overs, hands kept on the floor. Strength and balance predict whether a person can use the more symmetrical, hands-free patterns. The hands help a lot: hand forces of up to ~60% of body weight on a handle cut hip demand >70% and knee demand >50% (secondary report). In older adults, the half-kneel is the most common self-chosen strategy, but a third use the quadruped push-up and a sixth roll over.

### Cited Findings
- Self-selected floor-to-stand in 34 independent older adults:
  - half-kneel 17 (50%), quadruped push-up 11 (32.4%), roll-over 6 (17.6%);
  - mean times 4.04 s, 6.26 s and 3.68 s respectively;
  - therapists most often teach the half-kneel, even though several strategies are used.
  — [Stutzenberger & Whited 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12452367/)
- Twenty older adults (65+): initiation strategies were sit-up (n = 12), side-sit (n = 4) and roll-over (n = 4). Six people showed two methods. Faster self-selected rise time correlated with walking speed (r = −0.641) and with five-times sit-to-stand time (r = 0.563). The authors note no one technique suits everyone. — [Burton et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9958992/)
- Bohannon & Lusardi (2004), 52 adults aged 50–90, found three techniques: asymmetrical side-sit to half-kneel pivot, quadruped push-up, and symmetrical sit-up / roll over the feet. Stages were initiation, weight transfer and transition to upright. Performance related to age, sit-to-stand time and single-leg-stance time (search snippet). — [Bohannon & Lusardi 2004 (Sacred Heart repository)](https://digitalcommons.sacredheart.edu/pthms_fac/43)
- Alexander et al. (1997) timed rises from supine, side-lying, prone, all fours and sitting, with and without furniture. Healthy old adults took twice as long as the young. Congregate-housing older adults took 2–3× as long as the healthy old. 24% of them could not rise without support (13% with support), and the most successful rose from side-lying with furniture support (search snippet). — [Alexander et al. 1997, JAGS](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/2027.42/111267/1/j.1532-5415.1997.tb03088.x.pdf)
- Strategy training (Hofmeyer et al. 2002) improved floor-rise ability and perceived difficulty in disabled older adults compared with controls (search snippet). — [Floor-Rise Strategy Training in Older Adults, Deep Blue](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/items/05a9d335-055e-4297-950f-f58863f6e482)
- Sitting-rising test (de Brito, Araújo et al. 2012), 2002 adults aged 51–80, median follow-up 6.3 y:
  - each of sitting and rising scores 5, minus one point per support used (hand, knee, etc.);
  - a composite score below 8 was associated with ~2-fold higher death rates, and each score point with a 21% survival improvement (observational).
  — [ESC press release](https://www.escardio.org/The-ESC/Press-Office/Press-releases/Ability-to-sit-and-rise-from-the-floor-is-closely-correlated-with-all-cause-mort); [ScienceDaily](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/12/121213085202.htm)
- Children aged 3–6 with symmetrical (more hands-free) upper-extremity rising patterns had higher grip strength (6.9 vs 4.4 kg), trunk-flexor strength (65.2 vs 41.1 N) and one-leg standing time (24.4 vs 14.2 s) than children using asymmetrical push patterns. — [Kuwabara et al. 2013](https://file.scirp.org/Html/31326.html)
- Arm assistance:
  - A powered handle trajectory cut peak hip extension moment by >70% and peak knee extension moment by >50%, with vertical hand forces up to ~60% of body weight (secondary web summary only; this is an active device). — [Omnicuris summary](https://www.omnicuris.com/medshots/daily_updates/robotic-mobility-assistance-standing-sitting)
  - Higher armrests needed more pulling force but gave lower hip and ankle moments; armrests placed further forward gave larger knee and hip moments (abstract). — [U of Toronto thesis on armrest configurations](https://utoronto.scholaris.ca/items/178f3ecf-57ba-4222-8dc8-9b804dd4abbc)
  - Pushing off the thighs lowered the lumbar extension moment compared with no arm aid (abstract). — [TU Delft thesis](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_68510b42-de9e-4422-aae3-c960f07ac83b)

### Inferences
- The older-adult literature confirms that the quadruped push-up (the character's "bear" route) is a mainstream human strategy, used by about a third of independent older adults. It is not a stopgap. It is slower (~6 s vs ~4 s for half-kneel) and relies on the hands longer. So the character's long hands-down phase is expected and can be slow, which also fits the project's "patience over speed" stance.
- Strength-to-weight decides how hands-free the final step can be. Weaker children and adults keep the hands on (asymmetrical push, quadruped). For a torque-capped body, the human evidence argues for two things:
  1. As a strategy, keep at least one hand on the floor or on the thigh into the extension phase.
  2. As a body change, if the extra hands-free rise is wanted, raise the hip and knee extensor caps relative to body weight × height (see the ~0.88 N·m per kg·m quasi-static floor from section 5), or reduce head mass.
- Because the half-kneel is unusable for this body, the human alternatives with evidence are the quadruped push-up (bear), the roll-over / symmetrical squat (sit-up through a squat), and the side-sit pivot. The side-sit pivot ends in a half-kneel, so it inherits the same problem.

### Gaps
- I found no study of floor rising with the arms forbidden, or on one leg, apart from the sitting-rising test scoring (which penalises supports but does not analyse mechanics) and Yoshioka's arms-free chair simulation.
- I found no peer-reviewed measurement of how much a passive hand push on the thigh or floor reduces knee and hip moments during floor rising; the arm-help numbers above are for chairs, armrests or powered handles.
- Several older-adult papers (Alexander 1997, Ulbrich 2000, Bohannon & Lusardi 2004, Hofmeyer 2002) were seen only as search snippets. Their full PDFs did not render.
