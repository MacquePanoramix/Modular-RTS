# Learned getting-up and fall-recovery controllers: what made them natural, smooth and human-like, and what that means for "any body"

Scope: learned (reinforcement-learning or learned-prior) get-up and fall-recovery controllers for simulated characters and humanoid robots, read for what made the motion natural or erratic, the regularisers and priors used, body models, compute, robustness, and anything about different bodies or strengths. Current as of 30 October 2026. Reading depth today: full text (arXiv HTML or ar5iv) for Tao et al. 2022, HumanUP v2, HoST, the cross-morphology get-up paper (arXiv 2512.12230), UniReLo, the H1-2 balance-principles paper, the state-dependent AMP paper, Embrace Collisions, Robot Crash Course, the Cosmo AMP paper, ControlVAE and MaskedMimic; the Frezzato ETS thesis (French, text extracted from the PDF). Abstract or search snippet only, and marked as such: VIGOR, RecoverFormer, the few-demonstration fall-safety paper, StableMimic, the perceptual studies, MorphCon, Generative GaitNet, ACE, Human2Humanoid, X-Morph, Wobbledoll, the GDC 2026 Embark talk.

## 1. Tao, Wilson, Gou, van de Panne, "Learning to Get Up" (SIGGRAPH 2022): exact staged method, which strategies emerged, static stability, the cast tests, compute, and follow-ups

### Takeaway
A mocap-free, three-stage method (strong body discovers, weaker body adapts, slow tracking of its own motion) whose naturalness came from limiting the body (final strength about 40–60% of default) and the clock (up to 5x slower), not from smoothness penalties: the authors found energy and velocity penalties ineffective or destabilising, and the only explicit anti-oscillation term was a hip-velocity reward. Policies usually settle on one dominant strategy (often rolling over first, kneeling variants), are statically stable early in the rise and dynamic at the end, and adapt to locked limbs by using momentum. There is no success rate, user study or human comparison; a 2025 re-implementation on a real-robot model was about ten times jerkier than a heavily regularised successor.

### Cited Findings
- Stages: (1) a strong character discovers solution modes, (2) the policy adapts to progressively weaker versions, (3) a policy reproduces the weaker get-ups at much slower speeds; no motion capture — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.00307)
- Strength curriculum: torque limits β^i × 𝒯 with β = 0.95, advancing when test reward passes ω = 60, ending when a stage needs more steps than a threshold; fixed low limits (60%, 50%, 40%) usually fail to discover any get-up — [ar5iv full text](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307)
- Naturalness wording: "we find the final torque limits to be correlated to the naturalness of the get-up motions"; final limits of 40–60% of default are "usually perceived as more natural" than limits above 70%; this is offered "as a subjective observation". The authors aim at "slow-and-weak" motions said to be "more representative of most human get-up motions", which is not tested — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307)
- Smoothness levers actually used: the curriculum "eliminates excessively aggressive and abrupt motions"; a hip-velocity term was added "to avoid oscillatory behavior" (an unnatural swift stepping); energy-cost penalties had "minimal effect" and velocity penalties were negligible or "leads to training instability"; no action filtering or jerk metric — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307)
- Rewards (all terms bounded in [0, 1], multiplied): discovery/weak stage R = r_head-height (head ≥ 1.55 m) × r_straight (torso up-vector ≥ 0.9 when COM > 0.5 m) × r_horizontal-COM-velocity (within ±0.3 m/s) × r_feet (feet ≤ 0.9 m apart); slow stage R = r_COM-height-tracking × r_orientation-tracking × (r_hip + 2)/3; a balance stage adds a pose term toward a hand-set standing pose — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307)
- Speed control: the slow policy tracks the weak policy's own trajectory retimed by κ ∈ [0.2, 0.8] (up to 5x slower), and κ can be set at run time; strength cannot be changed at run time (fixed by the curriculum) — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307)
- Strategies (text plus figures): exploration with the strong body can get stuck in "variants of a kneeling motion"; policies usually share a final stand-up strategy and "the character commonly first rolls over, then gets up" when starting in the opposite orientation; some policies use different strategies for supine and prone — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307); "a dominant get-up mode, such as always first reverting to a prone position" — [arXiv abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.00307)
- Static stability: motions "often have significant static stability, i.e., they can be fully paused at many points in time" (done by repeating a reference state); they are more stable at the start and dynamic near the end, where "the character loses balance when asked to pause" — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307)
- Cast tests: the left elbow and right knee are locked straight (joints removed from state and action, no cast geometry); the character "relies on the remaining limbs to gain momentum" and uses the cast limbs for balance at some stages; a one-armed variant pushes with one arm — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307)
- Body and compute (earlier notes): 19 parts, 21 DoF, 38.3 kg, about 1.5 m, capsules, no toes or fingers; MuJoCo 800 Hz, policy 40 Hz; PD with kp = torque limit, kd = kp/10; SAC; roughly 24 h per stage on a 32-core CPU with an RTX 2070 — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307). Derived from 40 Hz: exploration episodes cap at 250 steps (about 6.25 s); the post-rise balance hold is 100 steps (2.5 s); no get-up durations in seconds are reported — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307)
- Human comparison: none quantitative; references are a web video of "52 ways to get up" and a physiotherapy case study; torque limits come from documented human values — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307)
- Limitations: a separate simulation of the weak policy is needed to make the slow reference; no user control over strategy; pausing only in statically stable poses; missing toes and fingers; chairs and other settings left for future work; results vary across seeds — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307). Code is public — [get_up_control (linked from the paper)](https://github.com/tianxintao/get_up_control)
- Independent re-implementation on a Unitree G1 (by the HumanUP authors): action jitter (third derivative, rad/s³) 5.39 supine / 11.73 prone versus HumanUP's 0.56 / 0.39; energy 650.19 / 1015.27 versus 91.74 / 69.98; described as jittery and jumping after getting up — [HumanUP v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12152v2)
- Follow-ups: Semantic Scholar lists 30 citing papers. Get-up or fall-recovery ones: HumanUP and HoST (RSS 2025), HiFAR (IROS 2025), Frezzato et al. (CGF 2022), "A Two-Stage Reinforcement Learning Framework for Humanoid Robot Sitting and Standing-Up" (Biomimetics 2025), "Robust Fall Recovery for Armless Bipedal-Wheeled Robots via Force-Guided Learning" (RA-L 2026), "Real-to-Sim-to-Real: Learning Agile and Robust Recovery Skills With Terrain Imagination via Adversarial Imitation" (RA-L 2026), "Robot Crash Course" and "Unified Humanoid Fall-Safety Policy from A Few Demonstrations" (both ICRA 2026); also PHC (ICCV 2023), AdaptNet (TOG 2023), "Too Stiff, Too Strong, Too Smart" (SCA 2023) and SMP score-matching motion priors (TOG 2025) — [Semantic Scholar citation list](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/arXiv:2205.00307/citations?fields=title,year,venue&limit=100)

### Inferences
- Tao et al.'s two naturalness levers map onto the director's wish that strength shape the motion: weakness changed the style (less dynamic), and the policy's own motion, slowed, became the reference. But strength was baked into each policy by the curriculum, not given as an input, so "any strength without retraining" would need strength sampled per episode and observed by the policy (an inference; not tested in the paper).
- The re-implementation's jitter (roughly 10–20 times HumanUP's) looks like the brief's twitches and one-frame flicks: a policy that only rewards height and uprightness, with no action-rate or bound terms, is expected to be jerky when moved to another body or simulator.
- Statically stable early phases and a dynamic final rise suggest that slow, pausable motion is achievable for the ground phases, but the last rise to standing needs momentum; forcing it to be slow makes it fail (the paper's pause failures). This is consistent with HumanUP refusing a 10x slowdown (section 2).
- The cast results are the closest published evidence that the body's own constraints change the strategy (momentum from the free limbs), which is the behaviour the director wants from varied proportions.

### Gaps
- No success rate, robustness-to-start numbers, or durations in seconds are reported in the paper; the strategies are only shown in figures and video, which I did not view.
- No test with altered proportions or masses (only locked joints and a missing arm).
- I found no character-animation follow-up that extends Tao et al. to strength or body parameters as policy inputs.

## 2. Real humanoid robots (2025–2026): which smoothness and safety terms made get-ups deployable; postures, terrains, success rates and speeds

### Takeaway
Every deployable robot get-up combined a relaxed discovery stage with a hardened stage, randomised fallen postures, and explicit smoothness machinery: action-rate and action second-difference penalties, joint acceleration, torque and power penalties, joint position and velocity limit penalties, posture-deviation ("style") penalties per joint, a bound on how far the PD target may move per step (HoST's action rescaler, annealed from 1 to 0.25), Lipschitz-style L2C2 regularisation, slowed tracking references (HumanUP, 8x), and occasionally exponential action filters or lower gains. Removing one of these made motion violent or oscillating (HoST: smoothness cost 9.52 vs 2.90 without the bound; real success 11/20 vs 20/20 without L2C2). Sim success is 92–99.8% on flat ground, real 78–100%; times to stand range from about 1.8 s to 6 s. 2026 work adds adversarial motion priors and balance-theory rewards, but hardware oscillations still appear.

### Cited Findings
- HoST (Unitree G1, 35 kg, 1.32 m, 23 DoF; RSS 2025) reward groups and weights: task (group 2.5): head height 1, base orientation 1. Style (group 1): waist yaw deviation −10, hip roll and yaw deviation −10 each, knee deviation −0.25 on ground (−10 on platform/slope/wall), shoulder roll deviation −2.5, foot displacement 2.5, ankle parallel 20, feet more than 0.9 m apart −10, feet stumble 0 or −25, shank orientation 10, base angular velocity while rising 1. Regularisation (group 0.1): joint acceleration −2.5e-7, action rate −1e-2, action smoothness (second difference) −1e-2, torques −2.5e-6, joint power −2.5e-5, joint velocity −1e-4, joint tracking error −0.25, joint position limits −100, joint velocity limits −1. Post-task (group 1): base angular and linear velocity, orientation, height, upper-body posture 10 each, feet parallel 2.5 — [HoST](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08378)
- HoST action rescaler: actions in [−1, 1]; PD target = current joint position + β × action, so β caps the per-step change; β starts at 1 and drops by 0.02 each time the head reaches target height, floor 0.25. A training-only upward pulling force follows the same pattern (200 N, −20 N per success, floor 0 N) — [HoST](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08378)
- HoST smoothness metric E_smth = Σ‖p(t+2) − 2p(t+1) + p(t)‖² over joint positions. Sim (ground / platform / wall / slope): full 2.90 / 3.39 / 4.66 / 5.31; without L2C2 4.05 / 4.47 / 5.29 / 6.32; without the bound 9.52 / 11.65 / 8.80 / 16.44 (energy 3.59 vs 1.35 on ground); fixed β = 0.25: 2.75 / 3.17 / 4.23 / 4.67 but "less natural" and wall success 84.6% vs 94.2%. Slower stand-ups are smoother — [HoST](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08378)
- HoST real robot: 20/20 versus 11/20 without L2C2 (0/5 on the slope), with oscillations; real E_smth with vs without L2C2: ground 1.83 vs 2.09, platform 5.06 vs 7.85, wall 7.22 vs 13.36, slope 1.94 vs 2.89. L2C2 (λπ = 1, λV = 0.1) bounds output change between interpolated consecutive states. No low-pass filter. Without bounds, training produced "violent ground hits and rapid bouncing" — [HoST](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08378)
- HoST phases: righting the body, rising to kneel, standing; PD gains (sim) upper body 100/4, ankle 40/2, hip 150/4, knee 200/6, raised on the real G1 (hip 200, knee 275) to close a torque gap; policy 50 Hz, PD 200 Hz sim / 500 Hz real. Extended to H1 and H1-2 "with minor hyperparameter tuning" (qualitative), where H1 showed more upper-body ground contact and posture deviations. Failures outdoors when standing from a seated position near obstacles. Training compute not reported — [HoST](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08378)
- HumanUP (G1; RSS 2025) weights, Stage I → Stage II: torque-limit penalty −0.1 → −5; DoF position limit −5 → −5; energy −1e-4 both; termination −500 → −50; DoF acceleration −1e-7 both; DoF velocity −1e-4 → −1e-3; action rate −0.1 both; torque −6e-7 → −0.003; foot orientation −0.5; soft body and waist symmetry −1.0 each (Stage I only); DoF tracking +8 (Stage II). No action clipping, low-pass filter or L2C2 described — [HumanUP v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12152v2)
- HumanUP slowing: Stage I finds a motion under 1 s, unsafe for hardware; Stage II tracks it slowed to 8 s; 4x slowing produced large torques and joint velocities and 10x did not converge. Soft symmetry beat hard symmetry (get-up 95.34% vs 84.56%; rolling 94.40% vs 75.53%). Without Stage II, jitter 13.70 and energy 1311.22 (supine) — [HumanUP v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12152v2)
- HumanUP motion character: "a continuous and more efficient whole-body getting-up motion" that uses the legs more than the arms (arm motors 25 N·m vs 83 N·m elsewhere); limitation: "unnatural hand raising for balance"; real G1 stands in about 6 s versus about 11 s for the manufacturer controller, which uses hand propping, sliding, squatting and waist tilting — [HumanUP v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12152v2). Real success: supine 78.3% vs 41.7% manufacturer, rolling 98.3%, on six terrains; posture randomisation from about 20K supine and 20K prone poses (earlier notes) — [HumanUP](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12152)
- HiFAR (Booster T1, 1.18 m; IROS 2025; earlier notes): sagittal joints first, then 3D with realistic torque and velocity limits; real 20/20 supine and 20/20 prone in 2.7–2.9 s — [HiFAR](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.20061v1). FRASA (kid-size, 5 DoF; earlier notes): early transfers showed tremors, fixed by lower motor gains and a higher inference rate — [FRASA](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.08655v3). OP3 soccer (earlier notes): actions exponentially filtered 0.8 old / 0.2 new; get-up 0.93 s — [OP3 soccer](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.13653)
- Embrace Collisions / Embrace Contacts (Zhuang and Zhao; CoRL 2025; G1): tracks human get-ups (CMU subject 140, KIT subject 3) and video-extracted motions through a transformer keyframe encoder; sparse task reward at keyframe times plus dense action-rate, joint-acceleration, energy and joint-limit penalties; three critics mixed by normalised advantages. Sim get-up success 94.30% multi-critic vs 65.13% single-critic, and 1.45% when trained on raw AMASS motions; the single critic shows repeated joint-acceleration spikes during get-up, the multi-critic none. No real success rates; in a breakdance sit-and-get-up the real robot needed its hands where the sim robot used mainly legs. The text calls single-critic "similar performance", which the table contradicts — [Embrace Collisions](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01465v1); [CoRL 2025](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v305/zhuang25b.html)
- UniReLo (Shandong University; arXiv July 2026; G1, 29 DoF): one policy for fall recovery and locomotion; PPO, 4096 envs, 50 Hz; three AMP discriminators (frame, 5-frame, 10-frame) as a gated style reward; regularisation action variation 0.015, joint velocity 2.5e-7, torque 2.5e-6, joint-limit 100, undesired collision 1.0. Sim success / time to stand (pooled postures): flat 99.53% / 2.35 s, gravel 97.18% / 2.13 s, 10° slope 93.28% / 2.76 s, 15° slope 89.42% / 3.57 s. Re-implemented baselines on flat: HoST 92.12% / 1.82 s, FIRM 90.56% / 2.58 s, HumanUP 89.85% / 6.18 s; on 15° slope HoST 52.91%, FIRM 40.30%, HumanUP 15.20%. Real-world tests (flat, gravel, grass, 10° and 15°) qualitative only; reference data for the AMP discriminators not stated — [UniReLo](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08922v3)
- Balance principles in RL (Poddar, Griffin et al.; arXiv March 2026; Unitree H1-2): capture point, COM and centroidal momentum as privileged critic inputs and reward terms; learns ankle, hip, stepping and "multi-contact stand-up using the hands, elbows, and knees". Isaac Lab 93.4% recovery over 10,000 trials, mean 5 s; MuJoCo 100% without pushes; up to 9 s for supine under push; hardware 10/10 from supine, seated, kneeling and crouched. "Transient high-frequency joint oscillations" in 3 of 10 hardware trials, attributed to damping mismatch, with action smoothing planned. Naturalness not evaluated — [arXiv 2603.08619](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.08619v1)
- State-dependent AMP (Lu et al., HKU; arXiv May 2026; G1): the AMP reward is routed to a recovery discriminator when tilt exceeds about 37° (|g_z + 1| > 0.6) and to a locomotion discriminator otherwise; recovery style comes from one LAFAN1 clip (fallAndGetUp2_subject2) retargeted by inverse kinematics; λ_amp = 0.5; hardware shows prone and supine recovery qualitatively; no success rates, timing or naturalness metrics — [arXiv 2605.18611](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.18611)
- Other 2025–2026 entries, abstract or snippet only: RecoverFormer (G1, MuJoCo) is push recovery, not getting up from the floor: 100% across 100–300 N pushes, 75.5% at +25% mass — [arXiv 2604.22911](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.22911); VIGOR combines fall mitigation and stand-up recovery in a vision-conditioned policy decoupled from locomotion (search snippet) — [arXiv 2602.16511](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.16511v1); a G1 fall-safety policy "from a few demonstrations" aims to "prevent falls when possible, reduce impact when unavoidable, and stand up when fallen", with no numbers in the abstract — [arXiv 2511.07407](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.07407); StableMimic reports 100/100 push-to-fall recoveries using perturbed resets around human get-up references (earlier notes, abstract only) — [arXiv 2608.02385](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02385)
- Robot Crash Course (Disney Research; arXiv November 2025; one 16.2 kg, 0.84 m, 20-DoF biped): learned soft, stylised falls to an artist- or user-chosen end pose (about 2 million sampled poses plus 10 artist poses); regularisers torque 1e-3, joint acceleration 7.5e-7, action rate 0.1, action acceleration 0.05, root acceleration 0.2; per-part sensitivity weights (head 4.0). Getting up is not tested; the authors suggest combining falling and recovery training as future work — [Robot Crash Course](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.10635v1)

### Inferences
- For the brief's one-frame limb flicks, the most direct published tool is HoST's bound on the per-step PD-target change (target = current pose + β·action), together with action-rate and second-difference penalties; HoST's own ablation shows the bound matters more for smoothness than L2C2 (9.52 vs 4.05 against 2.90). An exponential filter on actions (OP3 0.8/0.2; the Frezzato thesis 0.2, section 3) is the cheaper alternative.
- For the twitching or oscillation: L2C2-style Lipschitz regularisation and correct joint damping. The H1-2 paper's hardware oscillations from damping mismatch suggest that PhysX drive damping and solver settings deserve the same scrutiny as the reward.
- For arms bent "as if broken": per-joint posture-deviation penalties (HoST's shoulder roll −2.5, hip roll/yaw −10, waist yaw −10), joint-limit penalties two to three orders larger than the smoothness terms (−100), and symmetry terms. Even with these, HumanUP kept "unnatural hand raising", so penalties reduce rather than remove odd arm use; a pose prior does more (sections 3–4).
- Speed: every learned real-robot get-up found stands in about 1–6 s; HumanUP's 6 s was deliberately slowed for hardware safety; a 10x slowdown would not train. The brief's 10–13 s is slower than any learned get-up found here except supine recovery under pushes in the H1-2 paper (up to 9 s), so the project's slowness probably reflects hesitation or retries rather than a deliberate tempo (an inference, not measured).
- Success: the ablations that most explain a 1-in-3 rate are no posture randomisation (HumanUP 30–65%), no refinement stage (24–28%), a single critic for many reward groups (HoST 0%, Embrace 65% vs 94%), and no style reward on hard terrain (HoST wall 0%).

### Gaps
- Wall-clock training time is still not reported by HoST, HumanUP, UniReLo or the H1-2 paper.
- FIRM (a UniReLo baseline) was not identified; VIGOR, RecoverFormer and the few-demonstration paper were read only as abstracts.
- The RA-L 2026 "Real-to-Sim-to-Real ... Adversarial Imitation" recovery paper was not found beyond its title.
- No robot paper evaluates human-likeness with viewers.

## 3. Character animation: ASE, PHC/PULSE, CALM, MaskedMimic, conditional-VAE controllers, Frezzato et al., and mocap style priors versus no-mocap methods

### Takeaway
Character-animation get-ups that look human come from a human motion prior: AMP with get-up clips, ASE and PHC recovering from random falls under a mocap style reward, ControlVAE with a LaFAN get-up clip, and Frezzato et al.'s policy acting inside a conditional-VAE space of LAFAN1 poses steered by height and torso-angle curves. The Frezzato thesis gives the clearest ablation: without the pose space the agent tries to stand by lifting its head and fails; with an unconstrained VAE and unlimited torques it gets up but looks "very robotic"; with realistic torques only the conditioned pose space succeeds. No-mocap methods (Tao) are plausible but judged only subjectively. None of these papers ran a perceptual study of get-ups.

### Cited Findings
- Frezzato, Tangri, Andrews ("Synthesizing Get-Up Motions for Physics-based Characters", CGF/SCA 2022; ETS master's thesis 2022): the policy follows input curves of vertical height and frontal and lateral torso angle (given 0, 100, 200 and 300 ms ahead) instead of imitating clips; a latent embedding of natural poses is learned from motion capture and conditioned on those features — [CGF abstract](https://diglib.eg.org/items/d9302d75-fe63-4a27-a476-d52b543d54a3/full); [thesis PDF](https://espace.etsmtl.ca/id/eprint/3082/1/FREZZATO_Anthony.pdf)
- Frezzato thesis details (read in French): conditional VAE trained on a LAFAN1 subset (walking, running, falling and getting up); actions are a latent pose plus a per-joint offset added to the decoded natural pose, with a penalty on the offset; an action-smoothing coefficient of 0.2 (after Bergamin et al. 2019) "contributed to improving" foot and hand placement on hard terrain; humanoid with 19 joints, 49 DoF, 65 kg; PPO with 16 agents, about 30 h per 100 million policy steps; Vortex Studio physics at 60 Hz, policy at 30 Hz — [thesis PDF](https://espace.etsmtl.ca/id/eprint/3082/1/FREZZATO_Anthony.pdf)
- Frezzato thesis on torque: realistic torque values were "very important" for natural motion; earlier experiments with large torques gave "very robotic and unrealistic" behaviour — [thesis PDF](https://espace.etsmtl.ca/id/eprint/3082/1/FREZZATO_Anthony.pdf)
- Frezzato thesis ablations: without the latent space the agent tries to stand by raising the head and fails for lack of plausible pose transitions; a standard VAE with no torque limits gets up but the motions look "très robotique et non naturel"; with reduced torques the standard VAE fails to learn; the height- and torso-conditioned VAE succeeds — [thesis PDF](https://espace.etsmtl.ca/id/eprint/3082/1/FREZZATO_Anthony.pdf)
- Frezzato data and styles: 13 LAFAN1 get-up references classified by the Bohannon and Lusardi (2004) strategies for older adults (quadruped push-up, sitting up, rolling to side-sitting, pivoting on the knees); 28 training curves including hand-edited ones and versions 50% faster and slower; curves can be extracted from mocap or drawn in an editor — [thesis PDF](https://espace.etsmtl.ca/id/eprint/3082/1/FREZZATO_Anthony.pdf)
- Frezzato robustness: terrain curriculum (200 successes per level; success = full rise plus 3 s standing); succeeds "very often" on slopes up to 30°; recovery after real falls sometimes fails or makes unrealistic transitions unless fall scenarios are in training; the author notes there is no precise metric for motion quality — [thesis PDF](https://espace.etsmtl.ca/id/eprint/3082/1/FREZZATO_Anthony.pdf)
- ControlVAE (Yao et al., SIGGRAPH Asia 2022): 20-body, 49.5 kg, 1.6 m character in ODE at 120 Hz with stable PD, 20 Hz control; data are four LaFAN sequences (walk, run, jump, getup; the getup clip is 3365 frames at 20 fps), about 30 min after mirroring; about 50 h of training on one CPU plus a 2080 Ti. When pushed far off the reference "it then automatically discovers a recovery strategy to get up, which is not included in the dataset" (note: a get-up clip is in the dataset, so the claim is about the strategy, not the skill); an unseen rolling motion makes it "only struggles on the ground"; no success rates or user study — [ControlVAE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.06063)
- ASE (2022; earlier notes): 10% of episodes start fallen; the 187-clip dataset has no get-up clips; it recovered from all 500 push-induced falls, mean 0.31 s, maximum 4.1 s; about 10 billion samples, about 10 days on one V100 — [ASE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.01906)
- PHC (ICCV 2023; earlier notes): a fail-state recovery primitive with reward 0.5 point-goal + 0.5 AMP style + 0.1 energy; fallen-start recovery 95.0% within 5 s, 98.8% within 10 s; no external forces; about 7 days on one A100 — [PHC](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.06456). The repository notes that PHC+, the model used by PULSE, "can getup from the ground but not walk back" (search snippet) — [PHC repo](https://github.com/ZhengyiLuo/PHC)
- MaskedMimic (2024): the paper text read contains no fall-recovery or get-up discussion; 16,384 environments on 4 A100s for about 2 weeks; backflips and breakdancing remain hard — [MaskedMimic](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14393)
- AMP (2021; earlier notes): get-up clips let the character recover from falls, and "the policy also discovers novel recovery behaviors that are not present in the dataset"; 100–300 M samples per policy — [AMP](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.02180). DeepMimic (2018; earlier notes): one get-up clip per policy (3.28 s face-down, 4.02 s face-up), 49–66 M samples — [DeepMimic](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1804.02717)

### Inferences
- The Frezzato design targets the brief's symptoms directly: acting in a learned space of natural poses removes broken-looking limb configurations and random flailing, the 0.2 action filter removes single-frame flicks, and realistic torque caps remove robotic overpowered moves. Its weakness for Wonder Gather is that the pose space was learned from one human body's mocap.
- The height and torso-orientation curves are a body-light way to impose human tempo and phase order (time to sit, kneel, stand) without dictating limb angles. Scaled by body size, they could let proportions decide where hands and feet go (an untested idea).
- Mocap-prior methods are smoother and more human for human-proportioned bodies; the no-mocap method (Tao) is the only one that has shown body constraints changing the strategy. No paper combines the two for get-up.

### Gaps
- CALM's and Won, Gopinath and Hodgins' (2022) conditional-VAE controllers' get-up behaviour was not checked.
- No get-up success rates exist for AMP, ControlVAE or MaskedMimic; ASE's push-test falls mix stumbles with full get-ups.
- The Frezzato CGF paper itself (rather than the thesis) was not readable (HTTP 403), so the paper's final numbers may differ from the thesis.

## 4. Motion priors: how they create naturalness, how much get-up data they need, how they cope with bodies that differ from the reference, and whether they can coexist with "proportions and strength visibly shape the motion"

### Takeaway
Priors produce naturalness in two ways: they constrain actions to a learned pose space (VAE/C-VAE latent actions) or they reward transitions a discriminator cannot tell from reference motion (AMP, ASE, CALM-type). Get-up needs little data: one clip (DeepMimic; the 2026 state-dependent AMP robot), the six LAFAN1 "fall and get up" sequences (13 references extracted by Frezzato), or none at all if a general style prior is combined with fallen starts (ASE). Body mismatch is a known source of artefacts (head-heavy Cosmo; a C-VAE that needs matching proportions) and is handled by retargeting to the target proportions, adding body-specific data and keeping the prior's weight moderate. A prior learned on one body pulls every body toward that body's motion, so it pulls against "proportions visibly shape the motion"; no paper shows a get-up prior that stays consistent across varied proportions. Licensing also matters: LAFAN1 is non-commercial and no-derivatives.

### Cited Findings
- Data for recovery priors: one LAFAN1 clip ("fallAndGetUp2_subject2") retargeted to the G1 by inverse kinematics drives the recovery discriminator in the 2026 state-dependent AMP controller — [arXiv 2605.18611](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.18611); ASE learned recovery with no get-up clips (earlier notes) — [ASE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.01906); Embrace Collisions tracks curated get-ups from CMU subject 140 and KIT subject 3, and fails (1.45%) on raw AMASS motions — [Embrace Collisions](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.01465v1)
- LAFAN1: 77 sequences, about 4.6 h at 30 fps, including "Fall and get up" (6 sequences), "Ground" (5), "Push and fall" (2) and "Push and stumble" (3); licence Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-NoDerivatives 4.0 — [LAFAN1 repository](https://github.com/ubisoft/ubisoft-laforge-animation-dataset). AMASS is limited to non-commercial use and its terms prohibit training networks for commercial purposes (search snippet) — [AMASS licence](https://amass.is.tue.mpg.de/license.html)
- Cosmo (UCLA, ETH Zürich, NUS; arXiv September 2025): an entertainment humanoid with "a disproportionately large head (16% of total mass)" (about 4 of 25 kg), protective shells and 28 DoF; CMU mocap retargeted via Rokoko to a rig with Cosmo's proportions, averaging human limb motion to fit; the human ball-joint hip did not match Cosmo's two-axis hip; retargeting ignored the shells, so feet clipped. AMP style reward r = log D(s): without a standing reference it showed "a nearly jumping behavior"; without extra data from a model-based whole-body controller on Cosmo it produced high-frequency stepping. An AMP weight of 0.4 balanced best; higher weights improved discriminator loss but lowered the safety score. Balancing scores across head masses 2.2–6.2 kg ranged 0.613–0.677. No get-up; no viewer study — [Cosmo AMP](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.05581v1)
- Frezzato's thesis notes, of a related C-VAE controller conditioned on the current state, that pose conditions must match the proportions of the mocap character and that a character larger than the mocap subject caused problems matching root height to the ground — [thesis PDF](https://espace.etsmtl.ca/id/eprint/3082/1/FREZZATO_Anthony.pdf)
- Mismatch fixes reported for robots (search summaries, not read in full): Human2Humanoid adds an end-effector loss on trajectories normalised to each robot's T-pose and physics constraints against foot skating and ground penetration — [arXiv 2606.03476](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.03476); X-Morph turns human motion into "kinematically plausible, intent-preserving robot references" for a quadruped, hexapod and quadruped-with-arm (abstract) — [arXiv 2606.30290](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30290); ACE (SIGGRAPH Asia 2023) learns adversarial correspondences to retarget human motion to non-human bodies such as quadrupeds and crabs — [ACE](https://asia.siggraph.org/2023/index.html%3Fpost_type=page&p=14494&id=papers_1057&sess=sess167.html)
- Gating a prior by situation: the 2026 controller applies the recovery discriminator only beyond about 37° of tilt — [arXiv 2605.18611](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.18611); UniReLo uses three gated discriminators at different time scales, and removing gating or multi-scale AMP lowered its success — [UniReLo](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.08922v3)
- Production use of a prior: Embark added adversarial motion priors to ARC Raiders' learned enemy locomotion because the animation "didn't look good enough at certain points", which "improved the quality a lot" — [80.lv interview](https://80.lv/articles/inside-the-magic-of-machine-learning-that-powers-enemy-ai-in-arc-raiders)

### Inferences
- A mocap prior from a human pulls Wonder Gather's stylised, player-made bodies toward human limb angles that may be infeasible for them (Cosmo's jumping and stepping artefacts are the robot version). The stronger the prior's weight, the more every body moves alike, which works against "proportions and strength visibly shape the motion".
- Ways a prior could coexist with body-driven differences (inferences, none tested for get-up): (a) put the prior on features that do not depend on proportions, such as Frezzato-style curves of normalised height and torso pitch/roll over time, leaving limb placement to physics and the body; (b) make the prior from the bodies themselves, such as Tao-style self-imitation of each body's own discovered motion, so style is "physics-made" rather than human-made; (c) retarget a handful of artist-keyed get-ups to each body's proportions and keep the prior weight moderate and gated to the ground phase, as the 2026 robot papers do.
- LAFAN1's NonCommercial-NoDerivatives licence and AMASS's ban on commercial training make the two most-used get-up sources questionable for a commercial game unless permission or other data is obtained (a legal question these notes do not settle).

### Gaps
- No study measures how an AMP or VAE get-up prior behaves across a continuous range of body proportions.
- No evidence was found on proportion-invariant discriminator features for get-up specifically.
- The CMU mocap licence was not checked here.

## 5. Any body, any strength: one policy across morphologies and strengths

### Takeaway
Only one get-up-specific cross-morphology study was found: a single CrossQ policy for seven kid-size robots (0.48–0.81 m, 2.8–7.9 kg), sagittal pitch joints only, simulation only. It exceeds 58% success on all seven and 80% on four, transfers zero-shot to unseen bodies at 17–86% depending on how well the training bodies cover the space, and rescued a robot that could not learn alone (0% to 81%). Body-conditioned controllers for locomotion (MorphCon 2019, Generative GaitNet 2022) show that one policy trained over a range of proportions, masses and muscle strengths can produce body-specific motion without retraining. Get-up success is sensitive to strength (HiFAR 60% at 75% torque; Tao no discovery at fixed 40–60%), so strength must be inside the training distribution. No adult-size or game-character cross-morphology get-up exists in the sources found.

### Cited Findings
- Spraggett, "Learning to Get Up Across Morphologies: Zero-Shot Recovery with a Unified Humanoid Policy" (arXiv December 2025; RoboCup Symposium): robots Bez1 (0.48 m, 2.82 kg), OP3-Rot, Bez2, Bez3, Sigmaban (7.80 kg), Wolfgang, NUGUS (0.81 m, 6.68 kg); no morphology identifiers, the policy infers dynamics from state (joint positions, velocities, desired positions, trunk angles and rates, head height, previous action); actions are angle changes for shoulder, elbow, hip, knee and ankle pitch every 50 ms — [arXiv 2512.12230](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12230v1)
- Same paper, training: SAC with CrossQ (built on FRASA), MuJoCo, 16 environments, 600k steps per policy, about 1.5 h on an AMD 3900X with an RTX 3090; randomisation ±10% mass and COM, ±15% friction, ±10% motor gains; reward = head height + gated torso pitch + 0.1·exp(−‖q̇‖) + 0.05·exp(−‖a_t − a_{t−1}‖) + self-collision term; start poses displaced up to ±90° (face-up, face-down, side) — [arXiv 2512.12230](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12230v1)
- Same paper, results: leave-one-out zero-shot 17–42% for six robots and 72 ± 21% for Wolfgang; NUGUS could not learn alone but reached 81 ± 6% when trained with others; the shared policy lost 18–20% against specialists on Wolfgang and Bez2 and under 15% elsewhere; with more training robots Wolfgang's zero-shot rose from 37 ± 16% (one robot) to 86 ± 7% (four), dipping at six; "continuous, overlapping coverage" helps while arbitrary diversity does not (a diverse three-robot set gave 16% or less); outlier bodies may bring "competing strategies"; adult-size bodies and hardware untested. The abstract's "86%" comes from the scaling experiment, not the leave-one-out table — [arXiv 2512.12230](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12230v1); [abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.12230)
- MorphCon (Won and Lee, TOG 2019, older): "A single parametric controller enables us to simulate and control various characters having different heights, weights, and body proportions" (search snippet; locomotion, not get-up) — [MorphCon PDF](https://mrl.snu.ac.kr/publications/ProjectMorphCon/MorphCon.pdf)
- Generative GaitNet (Park et al., SIGGRAPH 2022, older): one pre-trained system covers a 618-dimensional anatomy space (mass distribution, body proportion, bone deformity, muscle deficits) on a 304-muscle model, where earlier work needed a new policy per condition taking "several hours to several days" (search summary; locomotion) — [Generative GaitNet](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2201.12044); [project page](https://mrl.snu.ac.kr/research/ProjectGaitNet/GaitNet.html)
- Strength sensitivity (earlier notes): HiFAR supine success fell to 60% at 75% torque limits — [HiFAR](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.20061v1); HoST handled payloads up to 10 kg (3/3) and 2/3 at 12 kg, and failed at 20% torque dropout — [HoST](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08378); Tao et al. could not discover a get-up at fixed 40–60% strength — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307)
- Body adaptation (earlier notes, snippets): AdaptNet reports that pre-trained policies perform poorly on modified bodies, especially the lower body — [AdaptNet](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.00239); XHugWBC drives seven real humanoids with one policy via morphological randomisation — [arXiv 2602.05791](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.05791)
- Per-character alternative (search snippet): a 2025 in-betweening system trains a separate RL controller per character that "reflect[s] the character's unique morphology" — [arXiv 2504.09413](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.09413). Physics retargeting to realistic body shapes (SCA 2018, older) notes a heavy character can lose balance getting up too quickly from a crouch (search snippet) — [SCA 2018 PDF](https://nmbl.stanford.edu/wp-content/uploads/sca18-fin.pdf)
- Embark's GDC 2022 talk "Walk Lizzie, Walk!" (Jorge del Val Santos) presented reinforcement learning to animate user-created creatures fully physically, with "animation emerging from the creature's anatomy", asking whether it can scale to a hundred creatures; whether one policy served many bodies is not stated — [GDC Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1027702/Machine-Learning-Summit-Walk-Lizzie)

### Inferences
- "Any body, no per-body training afterwards" corresponds, in the literature, to one policy trained across a sampled distribution of bodies and strengths covering the whole customisation range, either told the body's parameters (MorphCon, GaitNet) or inferring them from motion history (the seven-robot get-up policy). The evidence says coverage must be dense and continuous, success drops at the edges, and outlier bodies dilute training, so the player's customisation limits and the training distribution must be designed together.
- Strength as an input is what would let strength visibly shape the motion (weaker bodies choosing slower, more supported strategies, as in Tao's weak policies and the cast tests). Without it the policy learns one compromise style.
- The seven-robot study was cheap (1.5 h per policy) only because it controlled five pitch joints of light robots; a full 3D get-up across bodies is a larger problem, and no cost figure exists for it.

### Gaps
- No cross-morphology get-up for adult-size humanoids or game characters, and none on hardware.
- No study of strength as a policy input for get-up.
- How Embark's creature system handled per-creature training was not found.

## 6. Production: shipped or announced games and engines where characters get up by learned physics

### Takeaway
No shipped game was found in which characters get up from the floor by a learned physics controller. Embark's ARC Raiders ships learned physics locomotion for legged enemies, needed adversarial motion priors to reach the quality bar, allows "magic forces" against falling, took a team of five to ten people years, and was nearly cut several times. Tencent's DeepMimic-inspired Wobbledoll hands off to a kinematic get-up animation. No Unreal Learning Agents, Ubisoft La Forge, EA SEED or NVIDIA shipped get-up was found.

### Cited Findings
- ARC Raiders (Embark): reinforcement learning is used for legged locomotion (foot placement, movement), drones use traditional control; "we can cheat—real robots can't have magic forces that lift them up when they start falling, but we can"; adversarial motion priors "improved the quality a lot" when animation "didn't look good enough"; it "took a team of five to ten people working for years"; the system nearly got cut several times for missing the quality bar; training uses controlled scenarios with randomised object placement, no online learning; recovery is described only as enemies being "designed from the start to correct themselves" — [80.lv interview](https://80.lv/articles/inside-the-magic-of-machine-learning-that-powers-enemy-ai-in-arc-raiders)
- GDC 2026: "Learning to Move: Physics-Based Enemy Locomotion in ARC Raiders" by Martin Singh-Blom, Machine Learning and Animation Summits, 11 March 2026 (search result; the session page returned HTTP 403) — [GDC schedule](https://schedule.gdconf.com/session/learning-to-move-physics-based-enemy-locomotion-in-arc-raiders/917319). - Wobbledoll (Tencent): a full-body ragdoll system inspired by DeepMimic, DReCon and UniCon; after a knockdown it can run a custom falling motion and then hand off to a kinematic get-up animation once the character settles (search summary of the article) — [80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/wobbledoll-developing-an-advanced-full-body-ragdoll-system/)

## 7. Honest assessment: does any learned get-up look natural to human observers?

### Takeaway
No perceptual or user study of a learned get-up was found, in graphics or robotics. Naturalness claims rest on authors' impressions (Tao), robot smoothness metrics (jitter, E_smth), or none. The nearest viewer studies are unfavourable to learned physics motion: a 2025 study of an RL safe-falling controller found viewers preferred motion capture on 6 of 8 aspects (RL won only on reactivity), and a 2023 VR study found controller-generated motion less natural than captured averages. A 2023 SCA paper argues learned policies are typically too stiff, too strong and too precise compared with humans, and proposes measurable checks.

### Cited Findings
- "Beyond the Ragdoll: Designing a Safe-Falling Controller for Physically Simulated Characters" (Boban and Sužnjević, GRAPP 2025): an RL controller trained to minimise external and internal forces when pushed backwards; questionnaire with 25 participants comparing it with motion capture on 8 aspects; motion capture preferred on 6 of 8, RL preferred only on reactivity — [SciTePress](https://scitepress.org/PublishedPapers/2025/132592)
- "Too Stiff, Too Strong, Too Smart: Evaluating Fundamental Problems with Motion Control Policies" (Xie, Xu, Andrews, Zordan, Kry; SCA 2023): DRL controllers raise "several concerns" about naturalness; proposes measuring policy stiffness (how the character reacts to perturbation), two baselines for strength to compare joint torques with human performance, and variability "to reveal the unnatural precision of control policies"; proposes "simple modifications to improve realism" (not detailed on the pages read) — [Roblox publication page](https://about.roblox.com/publications/too-stiff-too-strong-too-smart-evaluating-fundamental-problems-with-motion-control-policies); [Eurographics DL](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.1145/3606935)
- "Exploring the Perception of Center of Mass changes for VR Avatars" (EGVE 2023): a physics-based controller takes motion, target body weight and height as inputs; perception experiments found average (captured) motions "consistently outperformed" controller-generated motions in naturalness (search snippet; page unreachable) — [Eurographics DL](https://diglib.eg.org/handle/10.2312/egve20231316)
- Study designs that could be reused (not get-up): UniPhys rated naturalness, realism and smoothness 1–5 with three raters per motion — [arXiv 2504.12540](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.12540); LYRIC used 14 participants with separate fidelity and naturalness questions and a "Neither" option — [arXiv 2609.19688](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.19688) (search snippets)
- Get-up papers' own evidence: Tao et al. subjective only — [ar5iv](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307); HumanUP and HoST use jitter, energy and E_smth with no viewer study — [HumanUP v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12152v2), [HoST](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08378); Frezzato notes no precise quality metric — [thesis PDF](https://espace.etsmtl.ca/id/eprint/3082/1/FREZZATO_Anthony.pdf); Cosmo judges "human-like" by discriminator loss and video — [Cosmo AMP](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.05581v1)

### Inferences
- The honest answer is: no learned get-up has been shown to look natural to viewers; the best ones are smooth by robot metrics and human-like by construction when they imitate human mocap on human-proportioned bodies.
- The "too stiff, too strong, too precise" diagnosis matches the brief's symptoms (aggressive twitches, flicks). Measurable proxies the project could track alongside the director's judgement: peak and mean joint torque against human-scaled strength, drive stiffness, try-to-try variability, joint jerk (HumanUP's third-derivative jitter) or HoST's E_smth, and time to stand against the 3–4 s human figure in the brief.

### Gaps
- No perceptual study of any get-up (learned, designed or captured) on physically simulated characters was found.
- The detailed findings and fixes of "Too Stiff, Too Strong, Too Smart" were not readable (Eurographics DL connection refused).
- A Trinity College Dublin thesis on motion perception and body-shape diversity in physics-based avatars was found only by title — [TARA](https://www.tara.tcd.ie/items/239eecff-d9e4-4123-b510-578bea12261a)
