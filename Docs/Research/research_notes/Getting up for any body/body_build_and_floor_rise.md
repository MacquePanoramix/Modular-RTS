## 1. Strategy taxonomies and how often each group uses them

### Takeaway
Two descriptive systems exist. VanSant's component scoring rates each body region from asymmetrical to symmetrical. Alexander and Ulbrich's "intermediate positions" (IPs) list the postures passed through. Across every group studied, the direction is the same: the less capacity a body has relative to the task (age, frailty, Parkinson's, amputation, stroke, a braced ankle), the more asymmetry, the more intermediate positions, the more points left on the floor (all fours, bear-walk, kneeling, a chair), and the longer the rise. Healthy young adults mostly roll partly and rise through an asymmetrical squat. Frail older adults pass through tuck, crouch-kneel, all fours and bear-walk, and roughly a third cannot finish without help.

### Cited Findings
**Healthy adults, and what moves a person along VanSant's scale**
- VanSant 1988, 32 young adults × 10 rises: only 25% used the fully symmetrical pattern (abstract; already in the earlier notes). — [VanSant 1988, Phys Ther](https://academic.oup.com/ptj/article-lookup/doi/10.1093/ptj/68.2.185)
- King & VanSant 1995, 39 non-disabled adults aged 20–28 (mean 22.7), 10 trials in each of 4 conditions: no ankle-foot orthosis (AFO), right solid AFO, left, both (abstract).
  - Without orthoses the modal pattern was push-and-reach (arms), forward-with-rotation (trunk) and asymmetrical squat (legs).
  - Every orthosis condition changed how often each pattern occurred toward more asymmetry, most notably in the trunk.
  - Locking the ankles (as a cast or rigid boot does) pushes healthy young people toward the rotation-heavy patterns of less able groups.
  — [EPMC-floor] (King LA, VanSant AF, Phys Ther 75:952)
- Thomas 1993, master's thesis, 33 older adults (mean 74.6 ± 6.5 y), coded with VanSant's and Luehring's categories: more active, stronger and more flexible subjects "used more developmentally advanced movement patterns in less time". **Older adults: intermediate positions**
- Alexander, Ulbrich, Raheja & Channer 1997, J Am Geriatr Soc 45:564 (abstract). Groups:
  - young: 12 men, 12 women, mean 23 y;
  - healthy older: 12 men, 12 women, mean 73 y;
  - congregate housing: 32 women, 6 men, mean 80 y.

  They rose from supine, side-lying, prone, all fours and sitting, with and without furniture. Results:
  - healthy older adults took about 2× as long as young adults;

  - 24% of congregate residents could not rise without support, and 13% could not even with support;
  - the congregate group was most successful from side-lying with furniture.

  - Ten IPs were identified.
  - The young were fastest and used the fewest IPs. Congregate residents were slowest and used the most. Healthy older adults were intermediate and used IPs common to both.

  - Congregate residents preferred "Tuck, Crouch-Kneel, All Fours, and Bearwalk".

### Inferences
- Across these studies one ladder appears, ordered by capacity, with each rung keeping more of the body on the floor for longer:
  1. symmetrical sit-up into a squat, hands-free (≈ 25% of young adults);
  2. partial roll, then an asymmetrical squat with a push-and-reach (the modal pattern of young and healthy older adults; ≈ 90% in Klima 2016);
  3. side-sit, then half-kneel, rising over the front foot;
  4. all fours, then bear-walk or a foot brought under (quadruped; ≈ ⅓ of independent older adults, 70% in Parkinson's);
  5. all of the above with hands on furniture;
  6. preparatory positions only: sit, kneel, tuck, ending in failure.

  A body's place on the ladder is a function of its capacity margin (section 3) and its range-of-motion gates (section 2), not of its age as such.
- A one-sided deficit (single AFO, amputation, hemiparesis) does not just slow the rise; it makes it asymmetric. The body turns toward the able side, kneels on one side and pushes with the other. A customised body with uneven limbs (one longer, one weaker) should therefore pick a side and rotate. It should not rise symmetrically.
- The armour and load data say more weight than more strategy change. Armour at 44% of body mass costs 2× the energy of walking but keeps most range of motion. A heavily armoured unit should mostly rise with the same strategy as an unarmoured one, slower and with more support points, unless the armour also locks joints (as the solid AFO did, which forced more trunk rotation and asymmetry).
- Note the human evidence about frail bodies: below a certain margin, the extra positions are not a style but the only feasible path. Ulbrich's non-risers reached sit, kneel and tuck and stopped there.

- Ulbrich 2000's IP-by-group percentages and Alexander 1997's per-position times are in PDFs that did not render. Only the abstracts were read.
- I found no floor-rise strategy study in obese adults, pregnant women, people in casts on the knee or hip, or people wearing armour or heavy loads. Military prone-to-stand timings exist in DTIC reports, but the server returned 403.

## 2. Proportions, mass distribution and joint range: which postures a body can use

### Takeaway
Feasibility is gated mainly by joint range:
- ankle dorsiflexion for a heels-down deep squat: ≈ 30–35° or more is commonly quoted;
- knee flexion: ≈ 120–130° for floor-rise paths, ≈ 157° for a heels-up full squat;
- hip flexion: ≈ 90–97°;
- trunk flexion, which a belly or pregnancy limits.

The share of people who fail the heels-down squat is substantial: 22.5% of young men and 11.9% of schoolchildren. Mass distribution decides how the remaining paths are used. Obese bodies carry more mass in the trunk, lean less, pull the feet back and load the knee about twice as much in a chair rise. Limb-length ratios (long femur against torso) matter, but less than ankle mobility: dorsiflexion explains 45% of forward trunk lean, and all segment ratios together add 15%.

- Trunk-flexion ability is thought to contribute to age differences in rising from supine, in a comparison of young and older women (snippet; probably Alexander et al. 1995). — [Deep Blue PDF](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/2027.42/111246/1/j.1532-5415.1995.tb05804.x.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Feasibility gates for a procedural chooser** (thresholds from the human data above; treat them as soft and scale them with the body's own range limits):

  | Posture or path | Needs | Bodies that fail it |
  |---|---|---|
  | Heels-down deep squat, and a symmetrical sit-up rolled over the feet | ankle dorsiflexion ≈ 30–35°+, knee ≈ 120–130°+, hip ≈ 90–97°, enough trunk flexion to bring the centre of mass over the feet | stiff ankles, long femurs with a short torso, a big belly |
  | Heels-up squat | knee ≈ 157° | stiff knees |
  | Half-kneel | large hip and ankle range on the front leg, a kneeling knee that tolerates load | bodies that are only stiff in the ankle; it needs no deep dorsiflexion of the rear foot |
  | Quadruped / bear | hands that reach the floor with the knees or feet under the hips; fewest range demands | the fallback when the gates above close; the share in the data rises exactly where knee mobility or strength falls |

- **Mass distribution.** A heavier trunk (obesity, armour on the torso, a toddler-scale head) moves the whole-body centre of mass up and forward.
  - With trunk flexion limited (belly against thighs, pregnancy, spinal protection), the body cannot bring the centre of mass over the feet by leaning. It compensates by pulling the feet back (50 mm in Sibella), widening the stance, and loading the knee about twice as much (0.75 vs 0.38 N·m/kg).
  - Once fatigued, it leans more after all, to spare the knee (Galli).
  - For a heavy unit this gives a visible order: upright and knee-driven when fresh, folding forward and swinging the trunk once tired.
- **Limb lengths.** Under similar range limits, a long-legged, short-torso body has to lean further forward to keep the centre of mass over a short foot. A long-legged body with short arms also loses hand contact earlier in a squat. Both push it toward kneeling or half-kneel paths.
- **Toddler-like heads.** The earlier notes found that a heavy head (≈ 21–25% of mass) favours symmetrical, hands-and-feet ("pike" or bear) paths with the hands kept down long.

### Gaps
- I found no study measuring how arm length, or the arm-to-leg ratio, changes floor-rise strategy. I found no floor-rise study of very tall or long-limbed adults.
- Drillis & Contini segment lengths as fractions of height were in the truncated part of the notebook and were not read. I did not use remembered values.
- The Hemmerich heels-down dorsiflexion value is unverified. The Chambers DXA percentages are a search snippet only.
- The Kasuyama ROC cut-offs for dorsiflexion and body weight are not in the abstract.

## 3. Strength, how weak bodies compensate, and capacity scales

### Takeaway
The knee extensors are the first limiter of rising from a seat. The hip extensors and plantarflexors carry more of the load in the half-kneel floor rise. Older adults rise from a chair at ≈ 80% of their maximal knee moment (young adults ≈ 42%), and impaired elders at the lowest seat they can manage use 97%. Rising from a seat without the arms needs a combined two-leg knee-extension force of about 46% of body weight in home-care elders. Relative sit-to-stand power below ≈ 2.1 W/kg (women) or 2.6 W/kg (men) marks limitation.

Weak bodies compensate:
- more floor contact and more intermediate positions;
- hands on furniture or thighs;
- feet pulled back and the trunk lean changed;
- trunk momentum;
- sometimes more than one attempt.

The sitting-rising test turns "how many supports did you need" into a 0–10 scale.

### Cited Findings
**How close to its limit a body works**
- Hortobágyi et al. 2003, young adults (mean 22 y) and old adults (mean 74 y) (abstract):
  - Share of maximal knee moment used:

    | Task | Young | Old |
    |---|---|---|
    | Chair rise | 42% (±19) | 80% (±34) |
    | Stair ascent | 54% | 78% |
    | Stair descent | 42% | 88% |

  - The old had 60% lower maximal leg-press moments, 53% slower knee angular velocity at peak torque, and 27% lower knee moments during the tasks.
  — [J Gerontol 58A:M453 abstract](https://biomed.gerontologyjournals.org/cgi/content/abstract/58/5/M453)
- Hughes, Myers & Schenkman 1996, young adults and functionally impaired elders (abstract; group sizes not read):
  - the required joint moment rose monotonically as the chair got lower;
  - the elderly used more of their available strength at every height;
  - at the lowest chair height they could rise from, the required knee moment was 97% of their available strength.
  — [EPMC-chair]; [BISp record](https://www.bisp-surf.de/Record/PU199611201430)
- Bohannon 2009, 31 home-care patients (mean ≈ 73 y), validation study (snippet): the combined isometric knee-extension force of both legs, normalised to body weight, separated those who could stand without the arms. The best cut-off was 46.3% of body weight (sensitivity 85.7%, specificity 88.2%; point-biserial r = 0.711). — [BISp record](https://www.bisp-surf.de/Record/PU201001000405)
- A Japanese elderly sample: knee-extension strength of 45.5% of body weight separated independent from dependent walkers (snippet). — [J Phys Ther Sci 9:19](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jpts/9/1/9_1_19/_article)
- In an experimental and simulation study, knee-extensor capacity limited sit-to-stand in weak muscles. Under extra load, a strategy change moved effort to the hips and plantarflexors (snippet; earlier notes). — [Maastricht University](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/en/publications/muscles-limiting-the-sit-to-stand-movement-an-experimental-simula/)

**Power-to-weight as the capacity axis**
- Alcazar et al. 2021, large European cohort, 30-second sit-to-stand power (full-text record):
  - low relative power cut-offs were 2.1 W/kg (older women) and 2.6 W/kg (older men);
  - the minimal clinically important differences were 0.33 and 0.42 W/kg;
  - relative power fell by ≈ 0.10–0.13 W/kg per year between 50 and 80.
  — [Alcazar et al. 2021, J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8350203)
- Other cohorts give similar cut-offs:
  - 2.5 (men) and 1.9 W/kg (women) in 1,369 older adults;
  - 2.53 and 2.01 W/kg, with declines of 1.0% per year (men) and 1.5% per year (women).
  — [Baltasar-Fernandez et al. 2021](https://doaj.org/article/d4d639cd036f4a0d9a689669f0c4c85d); [Bedfordshire record](https://researchportal.beds.ac.uk/p/en/research-outputs/cut-off-points-for-low-relative-30-s-sit-to-stand-power-and-their)
- Floor-rise time follows strength and power measures:
  - knee-extension power during five-times sit-to-stand, r = −0.46; age, r = 0.52; five-times sit-to-stand time, r = 0.56 (n = 34, full text);
  - walking speed, r = −0.641; five-times sit-to-stand, r = 0.563 (n = 20, earlier notes);
  - grip, r = −0.30; gait speed, r = −0.61 (n = 53).
  — [Stutzenberger & Whited 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12452367/); [Burton et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9958992/); [Klima 2016](https://digitalcommons.sacredheart.edu/pthms_fac/156)
- Children aged 3–6 who rose with symmetrical (hands-free) arm patterns had higher grip (6.9 vs 4.4 kg), trunk-flexor strength (65.2 vs 41.1 N) and one-leg stance time (24.4 vs 14.2 s) (full text; earlier notes). — [Kuwabara et al. 2013](https://file.scirp.org/Html/31326.html)
- Training: in a meta-analysis of 41 studies of people aged ≥ 60, randomised trials showed no significant change in floor-rise time (mean difference −0.43 s, 95% CI −1.38 to 0.51). Resistance training trended toward improvement (−0.81 s) (abstract). — [EPMC-floor] (Burton et al. 2020, Disabil Rehabil)

**The sitting-rising test as a capacity scale**
- How it is scored: sitting down and rising each score 5 points.
  - One point is subtracted for each support used: hand, forearm, knee, or side of the leg.
  - Half a point is subtracted for visible unsteadiness.
  - Araújo, the test's designer, says the main determinant is "muscle power to body weight" (opinion, press).
  — [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/healthmain/can-you-do-simple-sitting-test-predicts-longevity-1c7588565); [Bottom Line](https://www.bottomlineinc.com/?p=2637)
- Brito et al. 2014, 2,002 adults aged 51–80 (68% men), median follow-up 6.3 y, 159 deaths (abstract):
  - adjusted hazard ratios across the lower score groups were 5.44, 3.44 and 1.84, compared with the top group;
  - each extra point went with 21% better survival.
  — [EPMC-floor]
- Sex- and age-specific reference scores were derived from 6,141 adults (Araújo et al. 2020). The tables were not retrieved. — [Wikipedia: Sitting-rising test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sitting-rising_test)
- The SRT correlates with flexibility (r = 0.296, n = 3,927) and with BMI in children (r = −0.62, n = 284); see section 2.

**How much the arms carry**
- Able-bodied older adults rising from a chair: peak hand loads averaged 16–19% of body weight (114–134 N) (Anglin & Wyss, snippet). — [BISp record](https://www.bisp-surf.de/Record/PU199912407641)
- In a method study, the vertical force through each arm during sit-to-stand was typically 20–30% of body weight (snippet). — [University of Bristol record](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/a-method-for-measuring-vertical-forces-applied-to-the-upper-limb-/)
- Paraplegic sit-to-stand with arm support: peak hand forces of 48–73% of body weight (2 subjects; snippet; the document's host is unusual, so provenance is uncertain). — [PDF](https://chemistry.iust.ac.ir/files/intc/pages/papers/walker2/4.pdf)
- From the earlier notes: a powered handle carrying up to ~60% of body weight cut peak hip moment by more than 70% and knee moment by more than 50% (secondary). Pushing off the thighs lowered the lumbar moment. — [Omnicuris summary](https://www.omnicuris.com/medshots/daily_updates/robotic-mobility-assistance-standing-sitting); [TU Delft thesis](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_68510b42-de9e-4422-aae3-c960f07ac83b)

**How slow and fast risers differ in the movement itself**
- Schwickert et al. 2016, sensor on the low back (L5), 14 younger and 10 older adults; values are median (IQR) (full text):

  | Measure | Younger | Older | p |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Transfer time | 4.2 s (3.8–5.2) | 6.0 s (5.5–7.0) | |
  | Peak vertical acceleration | 4.7 m/s² | 3.5 m/s² | 0.009 |
  | Peak vertical velocity | 1.1 m/s | 0.8 m/s | 0.011 |

  Smoothness and fluency differed significantly. The authors read the older pattern as "broken-up" movement. — [Schwickert et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5017442/)

### Inferences
- **A model variable that matches the human evidence.** For each candidate path and phase, compute the reserve r = required joint moment ÷ available moment, joint by joint. Human anchors for the knee in a chair rise:
  - r ≈ 0.4: young, fast, symmetrical;
  - r ≈ 0.8: old, slower, more support;
  - r ≈ 0.97: at the limit, the lowest seat still possible;
  - r > 1: needs arms or furniture, or fails.

  The bands below are a design heuristic built on those anchors and the strategy ladder in section 1, not measured thresholds:
  - r < 0.5: symmetrical, hands-free (a squat or sit-up);
  - 0.5–0.8: asymmetrical, one hand pushes, half-kneel;
  - 0.8–1.0: quadruped or bear, two-hand push, hands on knees, more intermediate positions, pauses;
  - > 1: furniture, or failure and another attempt.
- **Strength-to-weight thresholds to anchor torque caps.**
  - Arms-free sit-to-stand needs about 46% of body weight in combined two-leg knee-extension force (isometric).
  - Relative power around 2–2.6 W/kg separates "limited" from "not limited" in older adults.
  - For the game, express each body's leg capacity in these normalised units, as torque per (mass × height) and power per kg. Customised bodies of any size then land on the human scale.
- **An SRT-like count as a readable output.** The number of support contacts used (hands, forearms, knees, furniture) is a direct, visible expression of capacity: SRT = 10 − supports, roughly. A weak or heavy body should visibly use more contacts, not just move more slowly.
- **Observable signs, each tied to the evidence above:**
  - Weak: more intermediate positions; all fours or bear-walk; hands on furniture or thighs; hands and feet repositioned more often; more trunk rotation and side-lying; lower peak vertical velocity (≈ 0.8 vs 1.1 m/s) and acceleration (≈ 3.5 vs 4.7 m/s²); a longer total time.
  - Heavy: an upright trunk at first, feet pulled back, a wide stance, a fast trunk swing (momentum), the knee doing most of the work; with fatigue, deeper forward folding.
  - Stiff (braced ankle): more asymmetry, mainly trunk rotation.
  - Pregnant (belly): a wide base, little propulsion, the trunk kept off the thighs.
  - One-sided deficit: an asymmetric half-kneel and frequent chair use (≈ 55% after transtibial amputation).

### Gaps
- I found no measurement of triceps or shoulder demand, or of hand force, during floor rising (quadruped push-up, hands on knees). The arm data are from chairs only.
- I found no strength threshold specific to floor rising, as opposed to rising from a chair.
- The Eriksrud & Bohannon 2003 threshold and the sample sizes for Hortobágyi 2003 and Hughes 1996 were not readable.
- The SRT reference tables (Araújo 2020) and SRT-by-BMI data in adults were not retrieved.

## 4. Joint moments and power by phase and strategy: which joint saturates first

### Takeaway
In chair and sit-to-stand rising, the knee extensors carry the largest moment (≈ 1.1–1.3 N·m/kg in older adults) and are the joint closest to its limit. The minimum combined peak hip + knee moment is ≈ 1.5 N·m/kg (Yoshioka), and lower seats raise it. In an instructed hands-free half-kneel floor rise, the demand shifts: hip-extension and ankle plantarflexion torques are significantly higher, and knee torque lower, than in sit-to-stand. In a deep squat, the soft-tissue contact between thigh and calf carries part of the load at the bottom. So the hardest moment comes just after the body leaves that contact, not at the deepest point. Mass carried in the trunk with limited lean (obesity) doubles the knee's share.

### Cited Findings
- **Chair rise, minimum demand** (earlier notes): across more than 5 million simulated 0.40 m chair rises, peak hip + knee moments summed to about 1.53 N·m/kg per leg, whatever was minimised. The ankle moment could be near zero (full text). — [Yoshioka et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1929086/)
- **Five-times sit-to-stand**, 34 older adults (mean (SD) peak moments, N·m/kg; differences between strategy groups not significant) (full text):

  | Joint moment | Half-kneel group | Quadruped group | Roll-over group |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Knee extension | 1.31 (0.52) | 1.10 (0.33) | 1.26 (0.55) |
  | Hip extension | 1.17 (0.66) | 1.13 (0.52) | 1.20 (0.64) |
  | Ankle plantarflexion | 0.52 (0.32) | 0.49 (0.18) | 0.49 (0.30) |

  — [Stutzenberger & Whited 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12452367/)
- **Instructed half-kneel floor rise without hands, compared with sit-to-stand** (24 participants with data for both tasks; full text):
  - "Ankle and hip demands were generally greater in the instructed FTS."
  - Knee-extension torque and power were significantly higher in sit-to-stand, and plantarflexion and hip-extension torques significantly higher in the floor rise (all p < 0.001).
  - Peak hip flexion was greater in the floor rise.
  - The exact values appear only in the paper's Figure 3.
  — [Stutzenberger & Whited 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12452367/)
- **Seat height:** the required moment rises monotonically as the seat gets lower (abstract). — [EPMC-chair] (Hughes et al. 1996)
- **Deep squat**
  - Lowering into or rising from a deep squat: the knee moment peaked at ≈ 0.93 N·m/kg, with tibiofemoral force up to ≈ 2.6 × body weight. The poster calls it a "flexion moment", so the sign convention is unclear (poster, snippet). — [ISB 2013 poster](https://media.isbweb.org/images/conferences/isb-congresses/2013/poster/ps2-10a.pdf)
  - Thigh-calf contact near full flexion produced mean moments of ≈ 3–4% of body weight × height in squatting and kneeling (snippet).
  - Soft-tissue contact reduced the knee flexion moment by ≈ 48% on average (review, snippet).
  - Foam knee supports cut the sagittal knee moment by 43–63% in baseball catchers (snippet).
  — [CDC Stacks](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/227130/cdc_227130_DS1.pdf); [Applied Sciences 2022 review](https://www2.mdpi.com/2076-3417/12/1/255); [Weill Cornell record](https://vivo.weill.cornell.edu/display/pubid30446216)
- **Where the weight sits on the foot:** moving the centre of pressure forward in a squat (from 44.4% to 53.5% of foot length) raised the ankle moment ≈ 30% and lowered the knee moment ≈ 6% (full text; earlier notes). — [JSSM 2022](https://www.jssm.org/jssm-21-341.xml-Fulltext)
- **Obesity, chair rise:** peak knee moment 0.75 vs 0.38 N·m/kg, and hip 0.59 vs 0.98 N·m/kg, obese vs normal weight (n = 40 / 10; abstract). — [EPMC-chair]
- **Relative effort:** knee 42% (young) vs 80% (old) of maximum in a chair rise; 97% for impaired elders at the lowest seat they can manage (section 3). — [Hortobágyi 2003](https://biomed.gerontologyjournals.org/cgi/content/abstract/58/5/M453); [EPMC-chair]
- **Arms:** 16–30% of body weight per limb in assisted chair rises (section 3).

### Inferences
- **Phase-by-phase demand map for floor rising.** This is assembled from the chair, half-kneel and squat data. The values are not measured for each floor phase.
  1. Roll or turn to the side or front: trunk rotators and flexors, low torque. Rotation is the cheap way to turn a symmetrical body around.
  2. Push up to side-sit or all fours: shoulder and elbow extensors, with the arm carrying a share of upper-body weight. By the chair data, up to ~20–30% of body weight per arm is ordinary.
  3. Bring a foot under, into half-kneel or bear: hip flexors, and range of motion.
  4. Half-kneel to standing: the front leg's hip extensors and ankle plantarflexors are the peak demands, the knee moderate. The kneeling knee's contact helps until it leaves the floor.
  5. Squat or crouch to standing: knee extensors are the peak demand, just after thigh-calf contact is lost. Leaning the trunk forward trades knee for hip; pulling the feet back and shifting the centre of pressure forward trades knee for ankle.
  6. Final extension and balance: hip extensors and small ankle corrections.
- **Which joint saturates first, by build.** Normalise each demand by the body's own torque cap at that joint.
  - An ordinary human proportion: the knee first in squat or chair paths, the hip or ankle first in half-kneel paths.
  - A heavy trunk with limited lean: the knee first.
  - Stiff ankles: the plantarflexors cannot help, and the knee or hip must take more.
  - A weak-legged, strong-armed body: it should stay in hands-down paths (quadruped, hands on knees), where the arms carry the deficit.

  This is the mechanical reason the frail favour all fours and bear-walk.
- **Size scaling.** This is my derivation, assuming the same proportions and muscle quality at every size. Demand moments scale with mass × length ∝ L⁴, and available moments with cross-section × moment arm ∝ L³. So the reserve r scales as 1/L: a geometrically similar body twice as tall has half the reserve, which is why N·m/kg demands scale with height. Tall or large custom bodies should therefore fall down the strategy ladder unless their strength is raised more than in proportion. Section 6 gives the animal evidence for the same pressure.

### Gaps
- I found no inverse-dynamics data for the quadruped push-up, the bear-walk, crawl-to-stand, kneel-to-stand, or the self-selected floor-rise phases. Stutzenberger did not report self-selected floor-rise moments in its text.
- I found no N·m/kg values for children's or obese adults' floor rising.
- Squat-lifting comparisons were not collected in this round.
- The sign convention of the 0.93 N·m/kg deep-squat value is unclear (poster only).

## 5. Timing: durations, stage times, pauses, fatigue, and perceived effort

### Takeaway
Rise time grows with lost capacity and with the number of intermediate positions:

| Group | Typical floor-to-stand time |
|---|---|
| Sedentary adolescents (fast tercile) | under 2 s |
| Healthy younger adults | ≈ 4 s |
| Healthy older adults | ≈ 4.5–6 s (half-kneel ≈ 4 s, quadruped ≈ 6 s) |
| Transtibial amputation | ≈ 10–11 s |
| Parkinson's | ≈ 15 s |
| Older adults before training (from supine) | ≈ 13 s |

Stage times can be estimated from rises started at intermediate positions. Older adults take ≈ 4 s from half-kneel, ≈ 8 s from side-sit and ≈ 13 s from supine; after training, ≈ 1.5, 4.6 and 7.1 s. Fatigue lengthens the rising phase and increases trunk flexion. No study measured pause lengths in human floor rising.

### Cited Findings
- **Durations by group** (whole rise; see section 1 for strategies):

  | Group | n, year | Start position | Time | Source |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Sedentary adolescents, 10–16 y | 62, 2021 | supine | terciles: < 2.0 / 2.0–2.6 / > 2.6 s | [EPMC-floor] (Tadiotto) |
  | Adults 20–50 y | 14, 2016 | 4 lying positions | median 4.2 s (L5; IQR 3.8–5.2; range 3.1–6.0) | [Schwickert 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5017442/) |
  | Adults ≥ 60 y | 10, 2016 | same | 6.0 s (5.5–7.0; range 4.7–8.9) | same |
  | Community older adults, 69 y | 34, 2025 | supine, self-selected | 4.7 ± 2.1 s (range 2–10.7); half-kneel 4.04, quadruped 6.26, roll-over 3.68 s | [Stutzenberger & Whited 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12452367/) |

- **Times from each starting posture, before → after training** (Seeley et al. 2026; 61 adults aged ≥ 65, cluster pilot randomised trial, 49 completed; abstract):

  | Start | Before | After |
  |---|---|---|
  | Supine | 13.1 s | 7.1 s (P = .001) |
  | Side-sitting | 8.0 s | 4.6 s (P = .046) |
  | Half-kneeling | 3.9 s | 1.5 s (P < .001) |

  After training, 100% of the trained group could rise from supine, against 63% of controls (P = .007). — [EPMC-floor]
- **Training and time:** Hofmeyer 2002's strategy training raised task completion but not rise time. Across trials, the pooled change in rise time was −0.43 s (not significant). — [EPMC-floor]
- **Fatigue**
  - Over a 30-second sit-to-stand test, 195 middle-aged and older adults with sensors showed (abstract):
    - mean power fell by 11.9 W (d = −0.66);
    - trunk flexion rose by 1.35° (d = 0.42);
    - the sit-to-stand phase lengthened while the stand-to-sit phase shortened.

    The authors call this only modest fatigability.
  - In obese patients, fatigue over 10 trials produced more forward bending to cut knee torque (abstract).
  — [JSSM 2026](https://www.jssm.org/jssm-25-529.xml-abst); [EPMC-chair] (Galli 2000)
- **Slow-riser kinematics** (Schwickert): lower peak vertical velocity and acceleration, more angular motion, more repositioning; see section 3. — [Schwickert 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5017442/)

- **Perceived effort:** this round found no new study of how observers rate effort by body build. The earlier notes cover how viewers read effort and weight from kinematics (Runeson & Frykholm). — `Docs/Research/2026-10-14_HowPeopleGetUp.md`, section 2

### Inferences
- **Stage-time decomposition** (my subtraction from Seeley's group means; it assumes the stages add up):

  | Stage | Before training | After training |
  |---|---|---|
  | Supine → side-sit | ≈ 5.1 s | ≈ 2.5 s |
  | Side-sit → half-kneel | ≈ 4.1 s | ≈ 3.1 s |
  | Half-kneel → stand | ≈ 3.9 s | ≈ 1.5 s |

  For a procedural model, a weak or untrained body spends its time fairly evenly across stages. Practice or strength shortens the last, effortful stage the most: 3.9 → 1.5 s, −62%.
- **Time from body capacity.**
  - Total time ≈ the sum over the intermediate positions used × a per-stage time that grows as the reserve r approaches 1.
  - Human anchors: young adults ≈ 4 s through ≈ 2–3 positions; frail adults ≈ 8–15 s through more positions plus furniture; Parkinson's ≈ 15 s.
  - The quadruped path costs ≈ 1.5× the half-kneel path (6.26 vs 4.04 s) in the same population.
- **Pauses.** Pause lengths are not measured in people. The defensible design is to tie rests to the reserve at the next stage: rest when r > ~0.8–0.9, retry after rest. The horse anaesthesia-recovery data in section 6 show long settle periods in the sternal (belly-down) position before standing, and one to several attempts. That is anaesthesia, so it is an upper bound on behaviour, not a norm.
- **What a slow rise should look like** (measured contrasts, not perception studies): a lower and longer vertical push, more turning and side-lying, hands and feet re-placed, more stops at intermediate positions. Not merely the same motion slowed down.

### Gaps
- I found no measurement of pause or dwell durations at intermediate positions in healthy or frail people, and no per-stage timing from motion capture. The stage times above are derived from group means.
- I found no fatigue study of repeated floor rises. The fatigue data are from repeated chair rises.
- I found no study relating observers' rated effort to body build or rise strategy.

## 6. Unusual builds and non-human bodies

### Takeaway
- **Short-limbed humans** (achondroplasia) invent their own paths: prone wide-abducted push-ups into sitting, kneeling-up on both knees, rolling prone and pulling up, wide-legged sitting and pulling up with the arms. All of these keep the hands and a wide base involved.
- **Heavy bodies** lean less, widen and pull the feet back, and rely on the knee and on momentum.
- **Very large animals** stand on straighter limbs because muscle capacity scales worse than weight. Limb effective mechanical advantage (EMA) grows with mass^0.25 and then plateaus above horse size.
- **Quadrupeds** show two body-plan solutions:
  - horses rise front first, throwing the heavy head as a counterweight;
  - cattle and sheep rise hind first onto flexed carpi (the front "knees") and lunge the head forward.
- **Righting animals** with too little strength solve the problem with shape (domed turtles) or with repeated, partly random effort that eventually crosses an energy barrier (cockroaches). Horses recovering from anaesthesia often need more than one attempt.

### Cited Findings
**Children** (earlier notes, briefly): head ≈ 21–25% of mass in toddlers. Toddlers use hands-and-feet (bear or "pike") and squat paths before the half-kneel. In children aged 3–6, the asymmetrical patterns go with lower strength and balance. The newer data are in Tadiotto 2021 (adolescents) and the SRT–BMI study (r = −0.62); see sections 1–2. — [body_and_strategies.md, section 1 and 4 sources](https://file.scirp.org/Html/31326.html)

**Achondroplasia** (short limbs, relatively large head and trunk)
- Ireland et al. 2012, a prospective cohort of 48 children with achondroplasia under 15 months at entry, as summarised by a patient-organisation guideline (secondary). Counts are the number of children using each strategy:

  | Transition | Strategies (children) |
  |---|---|
  | Lying → sitting | prone with the legs widely abducted, pushing up through the arms and extending backward into upright sitting (29–30); rolling onto one side (9); other (3) |
  | Sitting → standing | kneeling up on both knees (21); rolling prone first and then pulling up (19); sitting with full leg abduction, then pulling up with the arms (count not separated); other (6) |
  | Standing → sitting | squatting down (21); dropping backward (13); spreading the legs (8); other (3) |

  Teaching aims to protect the lower spine during transitions. — [Beyond Achondroplasia guideline](https://www.beyondachondroplasia.org/en/library/guidelines/123-lying-to-sitting-to-standing-up-in-achondroplasia)
- Developmental charts list milestones specific to achondroplasia: "Snow plough", "Reverse snow plough", "Commando crawl" and "Bear walking". Children with achondroplasia show "unique movement strategies" that seem to compensate for their biomechanics, and their gross-motor development is delayed (chart; abstract). — [Johns Hopkins development chart](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/-/media/institute-genetic-medicine/achondroplasia-development-chart.pdf); [Adelaide cohort record](https://digital.library.adelaide.edu.au/items/9f3320be-ce13-43a2-b07c-8d392901b61d)

**Very heavy bodies:** obesity (Matrangola, Sibella, Galli, Bollinger) in section 2; armour and load in section 1.

**Size scaling across animals**
- Biewener's 1989 work: larger mammals stand more upright, raising limb EMA roughly ∝ body mass^0.25. Peak bone stresses stay similar from 0.1 to 300 kg, with a safety factor of 2–4 (review). — [Biewener 2005, J Exp Biol, "Biomechanical consequences of scaling"](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article-split/208/9/1665/9381/Biomechanical-consequences-of-scaling)
- A 2021 giraffe study notes that EMA plateaus or even falls in mammals larger than a horse (preprint). — [bioRxiv 2021](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2021.04.29.441773v1.full.pdf)

**Large quadrupeds getting up**
- Horse, rising forelimbs first:
  - it rolls onto the sternum with the legs tucked, raises the head and starts unfolding the forelegs;
  - it then "swiftly and forcefully throws his head down and forward" to unload and lift the hindquarters (magazine, not peer-reviewed).
  - A veterinary wiki: about 55% of a horse's weight rests on the forelimbs, and raising the head moves the centre of gravity backward (caudally), off the forelimbs (secondary).
  — [EquiSearch, "Rolling and Rising"](https://equisearch.com/articles/eqrolling3410/); [WikiVet](https://en.wikivet.net/Special:MobileDiff/159419...159431)
- Cattle and sheep "raise themselves up on to their hindlimbs first, then on to their flexed carpuses" (secondary). Cows lunge the head forward when rising:
  - extension guidance asks for 30–44 inches of space ahead of the resting front knee;
  - in Swiss free-stall barns, forward lunge space of 55–70 cm was judged insufficient, and open partitions with a high, flexible neck strap reduced "staggered head lunge movements" (Brouwers et al. 2024, abstract);
  - on pasture, 73.7% of rises were followed by one or more steps forward (student thesis).
  — [WikiVet](https://en.wikivet.net/Special:MobileDiff/159419...159431); [University of Kentucky extension PDF](https://afs.ca.uky.edu/files/minimizing_emergency_calls_from_freestall_housed_cows.pdf); [Brouwers et al. 2024, Animal](https://doaj.org/article/c3769ffb3fde47dc9c2eec393603b377); [Utrecht student thesis](https://studenttheses.uu.nl/handle/20.500.12932/7281)
- Giraffe: "throws its neck back for impetus" to hoist the forequarters onto the "foreknees". Rising takes longer because of the mass and long legs (online forum comment, low quality). — [Snapshot Serengeti forum](https://talk.snapshotserengeti.org/boards/BSG0000007/discussions/DSG0001njc)
- Elephants: no description of the rising sequence was found.
  - A zoo case report notes that physically handicapped elephants may struggle with the sequence of lying down and getting up (snippet).
  - A zoo said that an elephant that cannot stand risks stress to its internal organs, which can be fatal (news).
  — [Schiffmann 2021 PDF](https://www.asesg.org/PDFfiles/2021/54-24-Schiffmann.pdf); [CBS Colorado](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/colorado/news/cheyenne-mountain-zoo-malaika)

**Attempts: horses recovering from general anaesthesia** (not normal rising; drug effects confound)
- Morphine study, 22 horses (abstract/snippet):
  - untreated horses needed 4.5 attempts (95% CI 2.7–6.2) to reach the sternal position, against 2.0 (1.4–2.6) with morphine;
  - time to standing was 31.3 vs 26.6 min.
  — [AGRIS record](https://agris.fao.org/search/fr/records/65dfed4663b8185d9cb0150f)
- Medetomidine study, Spanish Purebred horses (abstract):
  - medians: 35–43 min lying on the side, then 5–6 min lying on the belly (sternal), then standing; 47–49 min total;
  - median 1 attempt to stand.
  — [Animals 2024](https://doaj.org/article/5eef287b46ef4577a0150440ffa21baa)
- Mean attempts to stand in other protocols:
  - 1.8 vs 1.4 with dexmedetomidine (p = 0.03);
  - 1.83 ± 1.17 vs 1.17 ± 0.41 with ketamine-midazolam;
  - 1.8–3.1 with rope assistance (39–67.5 min to standing).
  — [BMC Vet Res table](https://bmcvetres.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12917-022-03350-0/tables/2); [Indian J Anim Sci PDF](https://epubs.icar.org.in/index.php/IJAnS/article/download/135343/53821/401889); [Ciência Rural](https://new.scielo.br/j/cr/a/ktHmtZDKJd9gDDj6c7J4sZP/?lang=en)

**Righting with shape and with noise**
- Domokos & Várkonyi 2008, a geometric shell model built from field data on many turtle species (full text not read; abstract-level):
  - highly domed terrestrial turtles "with short limbs and necks have virtually no active control", and shape does the righting (some shells are monostatic: one stable resting position);
  - flat aquatic turtles right themselves by "pressing their heads against the ground with muscular necks".
  — [Domokos & Várkonyi 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2562404)
- Othayoth & Li 2021, cockroaches and a robot righting from the back on the ground (abstract/snippet):
  - wing pushing alone cannot clear the pitch (somersault) barrier;
  - opening the wings lowers the roll barrier, so the small kinetic-energy fluctuations from leg flailing can tip the body over it, probabilistically;
  - the barriers are ≈ 7× the mechanical energy of one walking stride;
  - righting became more likely and faster as the fluctuation grew.
  — [eLife 2021](https://elifesciences.org/articles/60233v1); [Dynamic Walking 2020 abstract](https://www.seas.upenn.edu/~posa/DynamicWalking2020/684-1043-1-RV.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Large or heavy custom bodies.** Biewener's scaling and the 1/L reserve (section 4) say big bodies cannot afford crouched, deep-flexion paths. They should rise through straighter-limbed postures, with more support points and longer stage times. Large mammals do this by standing upright. The obese use more upright, knee-driven chair rises and pull the feet back.
- **Front-heavy vs rear-heavy bodies.** A horse-like body, heavy at the front with a long, heavy head and neck, can use the head as a counterweight: lift it to unload the forelegs and unfold them, then throw it forward and down to unload the hind end. A body heavy at the rear and able to kneel on its forelimbs (cattle) rises hind first onto its "knees" and needs forward room to lunge the head. A procedural chooser could pick front-first or hind-first from where the centre of mass sits relative to the limb pairs, and from which pair has the larger torque reserve.
- **Short-limbed, big-headed humanoids** (toddler, achondroplasia-like). Expect paths that keep the hands down long and use a wide base: prone push-up into a wide-legged sit, kneeling up on both knees, pulling up on a support. Their short limbs mean little vertical travel, but short lever arms and a top-heavy trunk.
- **Bodies that cannot muscle through.** The cockroach evidence supports a model in which a body below its required reserve can still succeed by repeated, slightly varied attempts that inject kinetic energy (rocking, flailing, momentum) until one crosses the barrier. Domed turtles show the shape-based alternative. Horses in recovery often need one to four or more attempts, after long belly-down rests. For a game body, a stochastic number of attempts, with rests that scale with the shortfall in reserve, has a basis in real bodies.

### Gaps
- I found no description of how elephants get up, and only a forum comment for giraffes. I found no joint-torque or timing data for horses or cattle rising normally, outside anaesthesia.
- I found no peer-reviewed floor-rise study of adults with achondroplasia or of very tall or long-limbed people. The achondroplasia data are a guideline's summary of one cohort.
- A search summary claimed that a retrospective equine study linked first-attempt success to carpal extension, head and neck alignment with the forelimbs, hind-limb position, coordination while belly-down, and a longer wait before trying. The study could not be located, so this is unverified.
- The saddleback-tortoise neck-length finding was seen only in press summaries and is not included above.
