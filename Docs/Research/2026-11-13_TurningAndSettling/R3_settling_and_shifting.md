Already on the October page and not repeated: shifts held >7.5 s versus fidgets out and back,

## The short of it

1. **What follows a caught push has hardly been measured.** Studies stop at the touchdown of
   the first step or at "balance recovered". No study was found that counts or times the
   closing steps back to the preferred stance. What is measured: sway is back to its resting
   size **2 to 3 s** after a jolt in the young (slower in the old); the heart speeds up by
   about **7 to 10 beats a minute at 1 to 2 s**, more for bigger pushes, and is back within
   **8 to 10 s**; labs leave about **20 s** between pulls for people to put the stepping foot
   back. **[unclear for closing steps; one study each for the times]**
2. **The young catch most pushes with one step; several steps mean a bigger push or a weaker
   body.** Sideways, multi-step catches mostly begin with a crossover, and only they bring the
   legs into collision. **[well established]**
3. **The comfortable stance:** heel centres about **17 to 19 cm apart**, feet opened about
   **10 to 14 degrees** between them, roughly hip width; very individual, and only roughly the
   same day to day (**15%** spread in the area of the base). **[well established]**
4. **Weight between the legs:** even on average, but in relaxed standing asymmetric most
   moments, in one of **two favoured** shares, swapping between them. **[one study]**
5. **Rates over half an hour of free standing (young adults):** a quick out-and-back fidget
   about **once a minute**; a shift to a new place **every 3 to 5 minutes** (about 2 cm of
   centre of pressure, intervals as variable as their mean); a slow drift every 5 to 9
   minutes; about **two events a minute** in all, whatever the load, shoes, eyes or floor.
   **[one lab, two studies]**
6. **They grow with time and discomfort:** over 4 to 6 hours, shifts and fidgets get more
   frequent from about hour 3, tracking discomfort. Some people mostly fidget, some mostly
   shift. **[well established that they rise; exact rates unclear]**
7. **Older bodies shift less and smaller** (about four times fewer transfers of over half the
   body weight). **[one study]** Whether tiredness enlarges sway: studies disagree. **[unclear]**
8. **A weight shift starts the "wrong" way:** to throw the body over one leg, the push under
   the feet first goes onto the other leg, for about a quarter of a second; side to side it is
   the hips that load and unload the legs. **[well established for the start of a step;
   inferred for a plain shift]**
9. **Alive, not robotic:** captured human sway makes viewers feel more for a character than a
   still body. **[one study]** Animators: never symmetric, no visible loop seam, extra
   movements on random 30 to 60 s timers. Their 4 to 8 s weight-shift loop is far faster than
   people really shift (see 5). **[animation guide]**

## 1. After a push is caught with a step

- **One step or several.** 12 young women (age 22) pulled backwards at the waist: at large
  pulls mostly one step; 12 older women (73) mostly several, shorter, lower and earlier
  (Luchies and others 1994, abstract). In 84 people of about 79, sideways-jolted at 13 rising
  levels, one step began at level 8 on average and several at level 11 (Batcir and others
  2022). **[well established]**
- **Which foot** (old people, sideways; Batcir 2022). Single-step catches began mostly with a
  side step of the unloaded leg (49%) or the loaded leg (26%); crossovers 8%. Multi-step
  catches began with a crossover in 56%. Leg collisions only in multi-step catches. What the
  later steps did was not described. **[one study]**
- **Push size.** In single-step catches, step length and speed grow with the push; start time
  and duration hardly. In multi-step catches the first step did not change with the push; the
  extra went into more steps and a longer path (Batcir 2022). **[one study]**
- **The arms** rise before the stepping foot lifts, at every push size, further and faster for
  bigger pushes (elevation about doubled from the smallest to the largest of three slips;
  young sped up with push size more than older) (Jeon and others 2022; 9 young, 10 older).
  When the arms come down was **not measured** (recording stopped at the step's touchdown).
  **[rising well established; coming down unclear]**
- **Sway settling.** After a 10 cm step down onto one leg, 15 young adults (21) moved their
  centre of pressure 20.7 cm in the first second, 6.1 in the second, 4.8 in the third, against
  4.3 a second at rest: settled by 2 to 3 s; 15 older (67) still above rest then (Kimura and
  others 2024). **[one study; a voluntary step-down, not a push]**
- **Heart (nearest to "a breath after the fright").** 20 young adults (24) jolted backwards by
  treadmill belts, 25 times per level, 8 to 10 s apart; only the hardest level made them step.
  Heart rate rose from about 82 by about 7, 8 and 10 beats a minute at 1 to 2 s; blood pressure
  at 4 to 5 s; both back before the next jolt (Siedlecki and others 2022). Breathing not
  measured. **[one study]**
- **The foot put back.** A lab protocol leaves about 20 s between pulls for the person to
  return the stepping foot and stand again (a Toronto thesis, seen only as a search extract;
  the scanned PDF could not be read). **[unclear]**
- **Not found:** a count of closing steps, their timing, which foot goes back, trunk
  re-centring, head or look after a caught push in standing. In walking, step length and width
  are normal again within about 6 s of a perturbation (PLOS ONE total-recovery-time study,
  seen only as a search extract).

**For ours (inference, not measured).** People do come back to their comfortable stance, but
nobody has timed it. What is timed: the body is quiet again within 2 to 3 s, the heart within
8 to 10 s. An order that fits all of it: catch step, arms already out in proportion to the
push; sway dies over 2 to 3 s; then, unhurried, one closing step (most likely the foot that
left the comfortable stance farthest) and, after a big push or several catching steps, a
second; a short rise in breath that grows with the push. A hypothesis for the Game Director's
eyes, not a finding.

## 2. The preferred, comfortable stance

- **Width and angle.** 262 people aged 19 to 97: heel centres 0.17 m apart, 14 degrees between
  the feet's long axes; wide spread between people; sex and age differ little (McIlroy and Maki
  1997, abstract). 150 young adults (18 to 30), barefoot, "comfortable and relaxed", three days
  over three to four weeks: heel midlines 19.4 cm apart (SD 4.1), feet opened 9.5 degrees
  (SD 10.2: some toes in, many out by 20); base area varied 15% day to day, shrinking slightly
  each session (Daffin and others 2024). 20 young men: 16.4 cm between inner foot edges (range
  11 to 26); sway least at that width and at 20 cm (Luo and others 2025). Called "about hip
  width" by later work (Bingham and others 2011, search extract only). **[well established]**
- **How individual.** Between people the SD is about a fifth of the mean width and larger than
  the mean angle; within a person, rank is stable (ICC 0.84) but exact size is not (Daffin
  2024). Older people stand narrower (Prado and others 2011). What sets it is hardly studied.
  **[unclear what sets it]**
- **Weight share.** 41 young adults, a force plate under each foot: quiet standing nearly even;
  relaxed standing swaps between postures, asymmetric most moments, two favoured modes, women
  more asymmetric (Prado-Rico and Duarte 2019, abstract). **[one study]**

**For ours.** Each miner can own its stance: about hip width give or take a fifth, toes out by
a different few degrees each, and two favoured weight shares (left loaded, right loaded), not
one 50/50.

## 3. Prolonged quiet standing

- **Three patterns** (Duarte and Zatsiorsky 1999, abstract; 10 young adults, over 30 min, free
  but not to step off): shifting, a fast move of the mean position to a new place; fidgeting, a
  fast large excursion and back; drifting, a slow continuous wander. Mean +/- SD intervals:
  shifting 316 +/- 292 s front-back (17 +/- 15 mm), 199 +/- 148 s side to side (22 +/- 38 mm);
  fidgeting 59 +/- 15 s front-back, 49 +/- 16 s side to side; drifting 319 +/- 173 s and
  529 +/- 333 s. Shift intervals are as variable as their mean: irregular, not clockwork.
  **[one study]**
- About **two patterns a minute**, unchanged by load, vision, floor or shoe sole (Duarte, Harvey
  and Zatsiorsky 2000, abstract). **[one study]**
- **Large transfers** (over half the body weight): old about four times fewer than young in
  30 min (Prado, Dinato and Duarte 2011, abstract). After 30 min standing, quiet sway is larger
  than before (Freitas and others 2005, abstract). Low back pain: fewer front-back posture
  changes (Lafond and others 2009, abstract). **[one study each]**
- **Hours.** 7 young adults standing 6 h (5 min seated each hour): weight shifts (over 10% of
  body weight on the dominant leg, held at least 7.5 s) more frequent by hour 3; fidgets (out
  and back in 0.75 to 7.5 s) by hour 5; five people fidgeted more than they shifted, two the
  reverse; the count depends heavily on the counting rule (Rekant and others 2019). Over 4 h,
  shifting rose and tracked discomfort (Wiggermann and Keyserling 2013, abstract; Cham and
  Redfern 2001 as cited by Rekant). **[well established that it rises]**
- **How one shift unfolds.** Feet side by side, side-to-side balance is the hips loading one leg
  and unloading the other; front-back is the ankles (Winter and others 1996, abstract). Before a
  step, the pressure under the feet first goes toward the leg that will be unloaded, for about
  240 to 250 ms and 4 to 5 cm, throwing the body over the other; the whole preparation is about
  half a second (Laudani and others 2021; 10 young adults). A plain weight shift should begin
  the same way by the same mechanics; no study of the order of hips, knees, trunk and head in a
  standing shift was found. **[start well established; order of parts unclear]**
- **Not found:** rates of moving a foot or changing the stance while standing.

**For ours.** The October rule (a shift every 9 to 26 s) is about ten times more often than
people's shifts (every 3 to 5 min) and nearer their fidgets (about once a minute). Truer: small
fidgets about once a minute; a real change of loaded leg every few minutes at very irregular
intervals; slow drift between; all more frequent the longer the miner stands and the more
tired it is; each shift begun by a brief press onto the leg about to be unloaded. Whether this
reads as alive at the game's camera distance is the Game Director's call.

## 4. Alive rather than robotic

- **Human sway matters to viewers.** Young adults (32 and 34, two experiments) rated a virtual
  character's pain face as more intense, and were more touched, when its body carried captured
  human sway than when it stood still (Treal and others 2021, abstract). **[one study]**
- **Few clips repeat visibly;** one answer is layered small posture variations plus personal
  balance changes (Egges, Molet and Magnenat-Thalmann 2004, search extract only).
- **Animators' guide** (Jirik 2026, MoCap Online blog): breathing loops 2 to 4 s; weight-shift
  loops 8 to 12 s, foot-to-foot over 4 to 8 s; secondary movements on a random 30 to 60 s
  timer, chosen at random; never perfect symmetry; robotic signs: no breath, symmetry, a pose
  or speed jump at the loop seam, foot slip, blends faster than 0.2 s (pop) or slower than
  0.4 s (float); tired idles lower, with a bigger out-breath. **[practice, not measured]**
- **Growth with tiredness.** Shifts and fidgets grow with hours and discomfort (section 3).
  Sway after fatigue is mixed: 28 young athletes after hard shuttle runs, women swayed less,
  men slightly more, not significant (Kozinc and others 2021); other work reports more sway
  after fatiguing muscles (search extracts only). **[unclear]**

### Stance (read)
- McIlroy & Maki 1997, Clin Biomech 12(1):66-70 (abstract via Europe PMC): 262 people 19-97 y; preferred heel-centre distance mean 0.17 m, angle between feet 14 deg; wide spread between people; age/sex differences small.
- Daffin, Innes, Stuelcken 2024, Musculoskelet Sci Pract 69:102896 (full PDF read): 150 people 18-30 y, barefoot, "comfortable and relaxed", 3 days over 3-4 weeks. Heel-midline distance 19.4 (SD 4.1), 18.8, 18.4 cm; feet opening angle 9.5 (SD 10.2), 10.1, 9.4 deg. Base of support area CV 15.2% day to day; ICC 0.84; area shrank slightly session to session.
- Luo et al. 2025, Front Bioeng Biotechnol 13:1538286 (full text): 20 young men; self-chosen width 16.35 +/- 4.2 cm (range 11-26 cm, between inner foot edges); sway least at self-chosen and 20 cm widths.
- Prado-Rico & Duarte 2019, Motor Control 23(4):553-565 (abstract via Europe PMC): 41 young adults, two force plates; quiet standing: small asymmetry; relaxed standing: on average small but moment by moment asymmetric, alternating between postures; two preferred modes of weight distribution; women more asymmetric.

### After a caught push (read)
- Luchies, Alexander, Schultz, Ashton-Miller 1994, J Am Geriatr Soc 42(5):506-512 (abstract via Europe PMC; Deep Blue PDF would not open): 12 young (22 y) and 12 old (73 y) fit women, backward waist pulls; large pulls: young mostly ONE step, old mostly several, shorter, lower, earlier.
- Batcir, Shani, Shapiro, Melzer 2022, BMC Geriatr 22:400 (full text via Europe PMC): 84 recruited, 79 y, lateral surface translations, 13 magnitudes rising. Single-step threshold level 8.2, multi-step 11.1 (of 13). First steps: in single-step trials unloaded-leg side step 49%, loaded-leg side step 26%, crossover 8%; in multi-step trials crossover 56%. Collisions only in multi-step trials. Later steps not described; recovery end "performing several steps", no criterion.
- Kimura et al. 2024, J Phys Ther Sci 36(9):481-487 (full text): 15 young 21 y / 15 old 67 y, step down 10 cm then one-leg stand. COP path per second: young 20.7, 6.1, 4.8 cm then 4.3 baseline; settled by 2-3 s; old slower (28.1, 10.0, 8.2, 7.4).
- Siedlecki, Shoemaker, Ivanova, Garland 2022, Physiol Rep 10(9):e15299 (full text via Europe PMC): 20 young 24 y, backward treadmill-belt jerks 300 ms, 25 per level, 8-10 s apart; only HIGH (0.62 m/s mean) made them step. HR up ~7/8/10 bpm (from 82) at 1-2 s; systolic pressure up at 4-5 s; both back before next jerk at 8-10 s. Breathing not measured.
- Hunt et al. 2017, J NeuroEng Rehabil 14:54 (spinal-cord-injury, not useful for settling): able-bodied push to foot-down ~614-780 ms (cited).
- Gap: no study found that measures the closing steps back to the preferred stance after a standing push. Protocols leave ~20 s between pulls "to return the stepping foot" (Toronto thesis, search snippet only; scanned PDF could not be read).
- Jeon, Wang, Bhatt, Westlake 2022, Brain Sci 12(7):953 (full text via Europe PMC): 9 young (28 y) / 10 older (62 y), slips at 3 intensities; arms rise before the first step lifts off at every intensity; elevation 25/33/52% (normalised elbow displacement) rising with intensity; young speed it up with intensity, old less. Measuring stopped at first-step touchdown: arms coming down not measured.
