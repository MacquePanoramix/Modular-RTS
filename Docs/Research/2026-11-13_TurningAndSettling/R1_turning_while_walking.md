## Findings

### 1. Step turn and spin turn

- Definitions **[well established]**: a *step turn* turns away from the foot that is down (the other foot steps out toward the new direction: an open, wide step). A *spin turn* turns toward the foot that is down: the body pivots on that foot and the swing leg crosses in front. [S1, S2, S3]
- Which foot is down decides it **[well established]**: when a turn is cued suddenly, the leg that is in front decides the strategy; with the right foot forward, a right turn was usually a spin on the right foot, a left turn a step turn onto the left leg. Some people used the step turn at every point of the step cycle, because it is easier. [S2: healthy adults, comfortable speed, cue by a random skin stimulus over 10–15 min walks, step cycle cut into 16 parts]
- The turn is laid in within one step of the cue (foot placement and trunk rotation), and in most people the walking rhythm is not reset. [S2] **[one study]**
- Stability **[well established]**: the step turn keeps a wide base of support, needs less twisting out of the forward plane and lower joint moments; the spin turn needs more range of motion about the vertical axis and more muscle work. [S3: 90° turns, self-chosen timing, small sample — authors ask for larger studies] [S2] [S1]. Two kinds of spin seen: a crossover and a pivot on the same-side foot. [S3]
- Where instability sits [S1, 15 young adults, 23.6 ± 3.1 y, 45° turns, own speed] **[one study]**: in the turn step of the spin turn (narrow, even crossed, stride width) and in the step leaving a step turn. Step turns were walked slower than straight walking; spin turns slightly faster, with the longest steps. Stance and single-support time longer than straight walking in both.
- How often each is chosen, as cited by [S1] (the original papers were not opened, so **[unclear]** until opened): young adults chose step turns in over 80% of 60° turns (Patla et al. 1991); healthy older adults (n = 19, 66 ± 4 y) mostly spin turns, step turns only for 90° at fast speed (Akram et al. 2010); 90° turns near 50:50 in Parkinson's patients and matched elderly controls (Conradsson et al. 2017); typically developing children almost all spin (Dixon et al.).
- Healthy older adults (abstract, n not given): spin turns were preferred for 45° and 90° turns at slow and fast speeds; step turns were preferred only for 90° turns walked fast. [S13] **[one study]**
- Turning in daily life [S18: 11 able-bodied adults filmed from the waist down on 4 simulated daily courses]: turning steps were 8% (office to car park), 35% (shop), 45% (office to office) and 50% (cafeteria) of all steps. **[one study, widely cited]** The game consequence: in a crowded workplace about every second or third step is a turning step, so turns are not a rare special case.

### 2. Order of turning: eyes, head, trunk, pelvis, feet

- The centre of mass is steered first (by where the foot is placed, and by a sideways roll of the trunk from the hips), then the head turns, then the rest of the body. Foot placement is preferred when the turn can be planned a step or more ahead; the trunk roll serves when it cannot. [S5: healthy adults, turns of several sizes cued at the start or one stride before] **[one study]**
- The head starts turning toward the new way about 1.1 m before the corner for turns of 45°, 90° and 135° (a fixed distance, not a fixed time); during the turn the head points further into the turn than the trunk, and that lead grows with the angle and peaks later for larger turns. Walking speed falls steadily as the angle grows. Fixing the turn radius only changed when the trunk began to turn. [S4: healthy adults walking round a post through 45/90/135/180°] **[one study, fits others]**
- Head and eyes lead a 90° corner by about 1 s, even in the dark (so it is planned, not only looking). [S8: 6 healthy adults, 90° corners, eyes open and closed] **[well established in kind; the 1 s is one study]**
- Before a change of direction the eyes always jump (saccade) to the end of the new path, and the head starts turning on average at the same moment as that saccade; the gaze stays there until the head has come round. [S6: 30° and 60° turns cued one step ahead or known in advance; n not in abstract]
- Head yaw leads body yaw by about 200 ms on a curved path; the head turns more than the heading change needs; at heel strike the feet already point toward the inside of the curve, ahead of the body's rotation. [S9] **[one study]**
- Head yaw leads the change of the body's path by up to 25°; the trunk does not lead. The head also rolls into the turn, up to 8°, ahead of the sideways acceleration (about 0.4 g at the sharpest). [S7: 5 subjects; 50 cm radius corners in 2 steps, 200 cm radius in 5–7 steps] **[one study]**
- Order eye, then head, then upper body, then feet, for turns toward lights at 45°, 90°, 135°. [S16: n = 5] Order head, trunk, foot rotation, foot displacement in older adults; 69% of their 40° direction changes were spread over two or more steps, 31% done in one step. [S15: 13 older adults] Pelvis follows the trunk; the order is reported to stay the same across speeds and turn sizes (Akram et al., cited in [S17], not opened). **[well established: "top-down" order]**
- En bloc: head, trunk and pelvis turn together as one block, with no head lead. It is the mark of Parkinson's disease, of people afraid of falling, and of healthy people whose eyes are made to hold still (search results; abstracts of Spildooren 2017, Cocks 2021, Ambati 2013 seen in a Europe PMC listing, not opened individually) **[well established that it is abnormal; details unclear here]**. For the game: a whole body rotated at one rate reads as ill or stiff; a healthy walker shows a visible lead of eyes, then head, then chest, then hips.

### 3. Steps and timing

- 90° step turns (7 participants, actual turn 80–83°): the approach stride was already slower and shorter than straight walking (the turn is prepared a stride ahead); the turn step differed in speed, length and width; the departing stride was still slower and shorter and speed had not returned to normal within one stride. Turning forces were larger than in straight steps; pushing off the dominant leg gave more propulsion. [S12] **[one study]**
- Over three steps of a 90° hallway turn (10 subjects, own speed): the step that starts the turn brakes more and pushes less than a straight step; the apex step pushes the body sideways (outward foot pushes inward); the step that ends the turn brakes less and pushes more. [S19] **[one study]** So a turn is: brake on entry, swing round on the apex, push off on exit.
- Anticipation sits in the step before the turn: the body leans backward (braking) and sideways before turning, and does so earlier when walking fast; angle (0/45/90°), pivot foot and speed all change it. [S14: 8 young adults] **[one study]**
- Steps needed: a 50 cm radius corner was walked in 2 steps, a 200 cm radius in 5–7 steps [S7]. Heading changes over the first two steps of a curve and is steady by the third (Courtine & Schieppati, as cited in [S17]). In 360° circles of 0.86–1.91 m radius at 30–40°/s, the outer leg's stride is longer [S17]. A clean table of steps for 45°/90°/135° walking turns in young adults was not found **[unclear]**; standing turns of 90/135/180° take more steps and longer as the angle grows (Khobkhun, Hollands, Richards 2022, Applied Sciences 12:5474 — abstract only seen).
- Speed: self-chosen turning on a 1 m radius was 0.96 ± 0.12 m/s versus 1.61 ± 0.22 m/s straight (10 adults) [S11]; speed falls steadily with the angle from 45° to 180° [S4]. Speed is tied to curvature by a power law (angular speed rises with curvature to the 2/3 power, i.e. tighter curve, slower walk) on ellipses; the exponent shifts with the shape of the path [S21]. **[well established in kind]**
- The rhythm usually carries on through a sudden turn [S2]; both legs keep one rhythm on a curve, with the inner and outer legs shifted by 7% of the cycle [S10].
- Steps for a 90° turn: healthy adults used two to five steps (Tillman and Zaferiou's earlier work, cited in [S25], not opened). 73% of late-cued 90° turns were step turns [S25: healthy young adults]. **[one study]**
- Most of a 90° turn's body rotation is in one stance: in 90° turns the pelvis was rotated about 60° (step turn) or about 90° (spin turn) by push-off of the turning step, against ±10° in straight walking [S22: 16 young adults, 21.4 ± 1.2 y]. The leftward push for a left 90° turn comes 2–4 times stronger than straight walking in the single support on the right (outer) leg, and the yaw momentum is made mostly in the double support before it [S25]. **[one study each]** So the body yaw is not spread evenly over many steps: it is concentrated in about one step, prepared by the step before and finished by the step after.
- Speeds in 90° turns (16 young adults): approach about 1.19–1.21 m/s at normal pace (1.26 straight); centre of mass path radius only 0.21–0.36 m (step turns tighter than spin turns); speed changed mostly by cadence, not step length [S22]. Healthy older adults (63 ± 9 y) slowed from 1.36 m/s straight to 0.96–0.97 m/s at the turning step [S24]. On a 1.2 m circle young adults walked 1.22–1.27 m/s against 1.52–1.55 m/s straight [S26].
- Daily turn angles: 76°–120° typical (Sedgman et al., cited in [S22], not opened); 90° the most common daily turn (Mancini et al. 2015, cited in Mellone 2016, not opened). **[unclear]**

### 4. The feet and the legs

- Hip twist actually used in a 90° turn (healthy older adults, n = 21, 63 ± 9 y, own speed) [S24] **[one study]**:

  | stance leg, degrees | straight | 90° step turn | 90° spin turn |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | peak hip external rotation | 2 | 8 | 15 |
  | peak hip internal rotation | 11 | 23 | 20 |
  | whole twist range in stance | 13 | 31 | 35 |
  | peak abduction / adduction | 3 / 9 | 7 / 3 | 2 / 11 |

  So a real stance leg twists about 30–35° in the hip during a 90° turn (SDs about 6–8°), against 13° when walking straight. The step turn swings the leg outward (abduction); the spin turn pulls it inward across the body (adduction). Inter-person spread is large (SD of peak rotation 14–16°).
- Spin turns need more twist about the vertical axis and more muscle than step turns [S3]. In the apex step of 45° and 90° turns the ankle evertors work more and longer, plantar flexors are active over more of stance, and hip adductors about double in the 90° turn [S27: 10 adults]. **[one study]**
- The ankle in the horizontal plane first behaves as in straight walking (it damps the foot's twist until foot-flat), then changes stiffness so the leg can follow the curved path of the centre of mass [S20: 10 subjects, 90° corner, initiation/apex/termination steps]. Numbers for how many degrees the foot itself spins on the ground (heel or ball) were not found in an opened source **[unclear]**. What was found: at heel strike the foot already points into the curve ([S9]) — the turn is put in by where and how the next foot is set down, not mostly by twisting a planted foot.
- Straight-walking toe-out for reference: about 18.5° ± 8° between the foot's long axis and the walking direction [S28: 11 young adults]. **[one study]**
- Inner and outer leg on a curve **[well established]**: the outer leg keeps its stride length, the inner leg shortens [S9]; outer foot swings faster; outer stance shorter, inner stance longer [S10]; outer stride longer [S17]. The inner knee bends more in stance, the outer ankle pushes harder (plantarflexes more), as fits a lean [S11: 10 adults, 1 m radius, 0.6–1.3 m/s]. On curves, the inner step width narrows as the curve tightens and the outer widens [S23].

### 5. Lean and balance

- The centre of mass and the trunk roll shift toward the inside of the curve [S9]. Sideways ground push: in straight walking each foot pushes the body toward the other leg; on a curve the inside foot pushes the body toward its own side and the outside foot toward the other [S11].
- Size of the lean (10 young adults, 22 ± 2 y): the peak sideways angle between centre of mass and stance foot was about 4.5° straight and about 6.9° on a 1 m circle at normal step length; it grows with tighter curves and longer steps [S23]. **[one study]** About 0.4 g of sideways acceleration on a 50 cm corner, with the head rolling up to 8° into the turn ahead of it [S7].
- The centre of mass is moved toward the new way first, by where the foot goes (when planned) or by a trunk roll from the hips (when the turn is late) [S5, S14]. In sudden turns the spin turn's narrow, even crossed, base is the least stable moment; the step turn's least stable moment is the step leaving it [S1].
- Speed versus tightness: people slow down for tighter curves (power law, [S21]); a 1 m radius is walked at about 60% of straight speed [S11].

### 6. What game and animation practice says looks wrong

- Valve's stride system keeps every planted foot on a fixed "footbase" so it lands and lifts without sliding; to take sharp corners they slowed the body (scaling root motion) rather than let feet slide; large changes away from the authored walking direction looked unnatural, so they chose clips close to the path's direction; they only switched clips while a foot was on the ground, to avoid the body appearing to change direction in mid-air. [S29] Their footstep prediction places the next foot from where the body will be, and how it will face, when that foot lands — the same idea as the game's "land for where the body will face".
- Industry blogs (seen only in a search summary, [S30], not opened) say a body yawing faster than the feet can follow causes visible foot sliding, and recommend letting the upper body twist first with the feet planted, then stepping once the twist builds up.

## What this means for the game's walk (comparison with the current settings)

Each line names the study it comes from; none is a Game Director decision.

1. **Body yaw rate.** The game yaws the body at up to 420°/s. Humans finish a 90° turn mostly within one step (pelvis 60–90° rotated by push-off of the turn step [S22]) at about 1–2 steps/s, so the hips turn at roughly 90° per 0.5–0.6 s — a few hundred degrees a second at most at the peak, and only during that one step; at other moments much less. Turning faster than the stance foot can follow is the classic cause of sliding or twisted legs [S29, S30]. A body yaw tied to the stance phase (yaw happens while a foot is planted and the other comes round) is closer to people than a constant-rate yaw.
2. **Planted-leg twist limit 62°.** Real stance hips use about 30–35° of twist range in a 90° turn, peaks about 20–23° inward and 8–15° outward [S24]. A planted leg twisted to 62° is about double what people use; at that point a person would already have stepped. This fits the observed "legs take weird positions". A limit nearer 25–35° with the step taken earlier would be within the human range [S24] **[one study, older adults]**.
3. **Order of turning.** Eyes → head (about 1 s or about 1.1 m ahead [S4, S8]; ~200 ms ahead of the body on curves [S9]) → trunk → pelvis → the next foot set down already pointing into the turn [S9]. The game's head (62° range, chest 35% of head) has the right order; check the timing: the head should start the turn about one stride before the hips, and point further into the turn than the trunk, increasingly so for 135°/180° [S4].
4. **Step vs spin by which foot is down.** When the turn is to the side of the planted foot, people pivot on it and cross over (spin); when to the other side, they step out wide with the free foot (step turn) [S2]. Young adults favour step turns for 60–90° (over 80% at 60° [cited in S1]; 73% of late-cued 90° [S25]); older adults spin more [S13]. The crossover spin is the less stable and more strained one [S3, S1] — legs crossing narrowly in a spin turn is real, but should be brief (one step).
5. **Speed and steps.** Slow down a stride before (shorter, slower approach stride), sharpest at the turn step, still slower one stride after [S12, S19]; tighter or larger turns are walked slower [S4, S21]. A 90° turn takes 2–5 steps, mostly done in one (cited in [S25]); a 50 cm-radius corner 2 steps, a 2 m radius 5–7 [S7].
6. **Lean.** A few degrees of lean into the turn (about +2.5° over straight walking on a 1 m circle [S23]); inner knee more bent, outer ankle pushing [S11].
