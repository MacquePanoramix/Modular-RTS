# R2: Turning right round (180 degrees) and what makes animated turns look wrong

Research notes, November 13, 2026. Complete for this pass. Gaps: no full text opened for a sharp (pivot) walking
180 turn with step-by-step numbers; Hase & Stein 1999 and the foot-sliding threshold paper were read only as
abstracts; no study of a standing turn to a sound from the side.

Marks: **well established** (several studies agree) / **one study** / **unclear**.

## 1. 180 degree turns while walking and from standing

### 1a. 180 degree turn from standing still (on the spot)

**Khobkhun, Hollands & Richards 2021** [S1] (opened, full PDF). 21 young adults (18-35) and 21 healthy older
adults; standing still, then turning 180 degrees left or right on level ground, paced by a cue to last 1.5 s
(fast), 2 s (moderate) or 3 s (slow); everyone ended with head, body and feet aligned with the new direction.
Inertial sensors, 100 Hz. Young adults (mean of each speed slow / moderate / fast):

| Measure (young adults) | Slow (3 s) | Moderate (2 s) | Fast (1.5 s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steps to complete the turn | 4.31 | 3.43 | 2.95 |
| Stepping duration, first step to last placement (s) | 3.05 | 2.22 | 1.72 |
| Yaw of the foot per step (degrees) | 73 | 75 | 79 |
| Head starts turning (s after cue) | 0.53 | 0.54 | 0.52 |
| Pelvis starts (s) | 0.65 | 0.60 | 0.57 |
| Leading foot starts (s) | 0.91 | 0.82 | 0.69 |
| Trailing foot starts (s) | 1.25 | 1.13 | 0.89 |
| Peak head yaw speed (degrees/s) | 137 | 181 | 272 |
| Peak head-ahead-of-pelvis angle (degrees) | about 16 | about 18 | about 22 |

Note: the PDF's table text is misaligned when extracted; the row labels above are matched by the paper's own
second table (per-group listing), which agrees. Read with that caution.

What it says in plain words:
- Order is top-down every time: head first, then thorax, pelvis, leading foot, trailing foot. **Well established**
  (this paper and the many it cites; also [S2]).
- The head leads the pelvis by only about 0.05-0.12 s; the first foot leaves about 0.15-0.4 s after the head
  starts, the second foot about 0.2-0.35 s after the first. **One study** for these exact numbers.
- A 180 degree standing turn takes about 3 steps when quick and 4-5 when slow; each step turns the foot about
  60-80 degrees. Slower turns use more, smaller steps. Older adults use more steps (4.8 slow). **Well established**
  direction, numbers **one study**.
- The faster the turn, the more the head gets ahead of the body (up to about 20 degrees). Above a head speed of about
  100 degrees/s, head and pelvis separate; below it they turn more "en bloc" (all as one). **One study.**

**Khobkhun, Hollands & Richards 2022** [S2] (opened, full PDF). 20 young and 20 older healthy adults; standing turns
of 90, 135 and 180 degrees, as fast as they could while feeling safe. Young adults (90 / 135 / 180 degrees):

| Measure (young adults) | 90 | 135 | 180 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Steps | 1.95 | 2.75 | 3.25 |
| Stepping duration (s) | 0.95 | 1.91 | 2.31 |
| Foot yaw per step (degrees) | 74 | 89 | 95 |
| Head onset (s) | 0.37 | 0.40 | 0.48 |
| Thorax onset (s) | 0.43 | 0.45 | 0.53 |
| Pelvis onset (s) | 0.50 | 0.55 | 0.58 |
| Leading foot onset (s) | 0.55 | 0.61 | 0.62 |
| Trailing foot onset (s) | 0.68 | 0.74 | 0.86 |
| Peak head yaw speed (degrees/s) | 124 | 152 | 173 |
| Peak head-pelvis separation (degrees) | 16 | 23 | 28 |

(Same table-alignment caution as above; row order matches the paper's list head, thorax, pelvis, leading foot,
trailing foot.) Older adults: 2.8 / 3.5 / 4.3 steps and smaller steps (61 / 71 / 80 degrees).

- A 90 degree turn from standing takes about 2 steps (one step with each foot) in young adults; 180 takes 3-4.
  **One study** (numbers), consistent with [S1].
- Larger turns: head leads by more, more steps, longer. **One study.**

### 1b. 180 degree turn while walking

**Madrid et al. 2024** [S3] (opened, full PDF). 10 healthy young adults (age 21-24, mean 23). Walk 5 m, turn 90, 180
or 360 degrees round a vertical rod, walk on at least 3 m; path and sharpness free. Motion capture 120 Hz. Turning
period = pelvis turning faster than 30 degrees/s, counted from the last toe-off before to the first heel strike after.
At normal speed (90 / 180 / 360 degrees):

| Measure (young, normal speed) | 90 | 180 | 360 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Turning duration (s) | 1.46 | 2.22 | 3.58 |
| Steps in the turn | 3.75 | 5.11 | 7.71 |
| Walking speed in the turn (m/s) | 1.13 | 0.99 | 0.88 |
| Cadence (steps/min) | 113 | 111 | 112 |
| Step length, inside leg (m) | 0.53 | 0.45 | 0.40 |
| Step length, outside leg (m) | 0.64 | 0.58 | 0.55 |

When asked to walk slower: 180 turn 2.49 s, 4.84 steps, 0.78 m/s, cadence about 92 steps/min.

- A walking 180 degree turn round a post takes about 5 steps and a bit over 2 s; speed drops by roughly 10-25%
  compared with a 90 turn. **One study** (small, n = 10), path not controlled.
- Cadence stays about the same through the turn; people slow by **shortening steps**, not by stepping slower.
  The inside leg takes clearly shorter steps (about 0.45 m vs 0.58 m outside at 180). **One study** for the numbers;
  inside-shorter is **well established** (also reported in curved-walking work cited by the paper).
- Caution: a turn round a post is a tight curve, not a sharp about-face; a sharp reversal (pivot) is likely fewer,
  braked steps (see Hase & Stein below).

**Hase & Stein 1999** [S4] (abstract only, through a secondary page; the journal page returned 403 and PubMed asked for
cookies). Young adults, sudden cued turns of about 180 degrees while walking. Two strategies, chosen by which foot is
in front when braking: turning toward the side of the front foot = **spin turn** (pivot round that foot, narrow base);
turning away from it = **step turn** (the other foot steps out wide, wide base, more stable). Foot placement and
trunk rotation both began within one step of the cue, timed to the opposite foot's strike, and the walking rhythm was
usually not reset. The early part of the turn looked like a quick stop. **Well established** (the step/spin
distinction is used by most later turning studies).

### 1c. Step turn or spin turn, and how often turns happen

**He et al. 2018** [S5] (opened, full PDF). 15 healthy young adults (mean 23.6 years), barefoot, preferred speed,
45 degree turns, both strategies done on purpose. Its introduction gathers earlier counts (secondary, I did not open
those papers): turning steps are about 20-50% of the steps of daily life; young adults chose the **step turn** in more
than 80% of 60 degree walking turns (Patla et al.); healthy older adults mostly used spin turns except fast 90 turns
(Akram et al.); about 50:50 at 90 degrees in older controls (Conradsson et al.); children mostly spin.
Its own result: the spin turn is least stable in the middle of the turn (narrow base, body mass passing outside the
foot); the step turn is least stable when leaving the turn.
- Turns are common, not rare: a fifth to a half of steps. **Well established** (several studies, secondary here).
- Which strategy is preferred is **unclear** (depends on age, speed, angle); young adults lean to step turns at
  moderate angles. **One study** (secondary).
- In a step turn the outer foot steps out to the side, so the legs never cross. In a spin turn the body pivots on
  the inside foot and the swing leg must come round in front; this is the turn where legs can look tangled if the
  swing foot passes through the stance leg. (Drawn from the definitions in [S4], [S5].)

## 2. 90 degree turns from standing

From [S2] (young adults, standing, turn as fast as safe, 90 degrees): about **2 steps** (1.95), stepping done in about
**1 s** (0.95 s). Order: head starts first (0.37 s after the cue), thorax 0.06 s later, pelvis 0.07 s after that,
leading foot 0.55 s, trailing foot 0.68 s. So the first foot leaves about 0.2 s after the head begins, and the second
foot about 0.13 s after the first. Each step turns the foot about 74 degrees; peak head speed about 124 degrees/s;
head gets about 16 degrees ahead of the pelvis. Older adults: about 2.8 steps, 1.6 s. **One study** for the numbers;
the head-first order is **well established**.

Not found: a study in which the person is *called* from the side (a sound cue rather than a screen). The cue in [S1],
[S2] is visual. Gap left open.

Plain-English picture of a 90 degree standing turn (young adult): eyes and head go first; a beat later the chest and
hips; the foot on the turning side lifts, toes out and lands turned about 70-90 degrees; the other foot follows and
lands beside it. Two steps, about a second. The body never twists all the way round over a planted foot.

## 3. What makes animated turns look wrong, and how games solve it

**Johansen 2009** [S6] (opened, full thesis PDF; master's thesis behind a GDC 2009 talk; later the base of the
Half-Life: Alyx system [S7]).
- Even small foot sliding destroys realism, because the planted feet are the body's main contact with the world.
  **Well established** (also [S7], [S9]).
- Turning in an arc: if a foot travels in a straight line from its old footprint to its new one, the **outer foot
  cuts across and can pass through the other leg**. Real feet follow the arc round; the fix is to bow the swing path
  outward, most at mid-swing. This matches the "legs in weird positions" seen in turns.
- People are "not vehicles": when they must go a new way, they **start stepping that way before the body has finished
  turning** (his example: a startled person turning to flee steps backwards first; drawn from Muybridge's photo series).
  Neither "drive forward on a sharp curve" nor "turn neatly on the spot, then go" looks right.
- Stopping: each leg is "parked" at its own stance time, one after the other, not all at once.
- Head look: eyes fastest, head slower, upper body slowest and only partly. **Well established** practice, matches
  [S1]-[S2].

**van den Heuvel, Cunliffe & Parker 2021** [S7] (Valve; SIGGRAPH 2021 talk paper; opened, full PDF).
- Each foot has a "footbase" that stays fixed while the foot is down, so feet never slide.
- The next footstep is predicted from where the body will be, and how it will face, when that foot lands, then the
  foot is flown there by IK. (The game already does this: feet land for the facing about 50 degrees ahead.)
- To slow down for **sharp corners**, they scale down the travel and shrink the step height with it, so stride and
  length still match; no sliding.
- Bending a clip far from its original direction "would yield unnatural movement"; they pick clips close to the new
  direction (motion matching).
- They only switch clips **while a foot is on the ground**, to avoid a change of direction in mid-air.

**Anguelov, Leblanc & Harris (undated slides)** [S8] (opened as a slide transcript; Hitman: Absolution and Reckoning).
- Separate start clips for -180, -90, 0, 90, 180 degrees and stop clips per leg, so characters do not "stop, then
  turn on the spot" in a robotic way.
- The planner must put the character exactly on the pivot spot before a turn clip; leftover error of up to half a
  footstep shows as sliding unless IK or path tweaks hide it. About 10% error per segment was judged invisible.

**Epic Games, Lyra sample documentation** [S11] (opened).
- Turn in place: the capsule turns with the controller, but the root bone is counter-rotated so the **legs stay
  planted**; the upper body aims through an aim offset. When the offset grows past a set angle (set per weapon), a
  turn-in-place clip plays and its own rotation is fed back, so the feet step round. Orientation warping bends the
  lower body to match the travel direction. This is the same idea as the game's 62 degree hip-twist limit with
  the body waiting for the foot.

**Pražák, Hoyet & O'Sullivan 2011** [S9] (NOT opened: Eurographics library and DOI resolver returned 403/redirect;
abstract read only through search summaries). Viewers noticed foot sliding of **about 21 mm or less** in most
conditions; cleaned motions were preferred whatever the amount of correction. **One study**, not verified in full.

Summary of what reads as wrong (from the above; mark: **well established** in practice, little formal perception
research beyond [S9]):
1. Feet sliding or spinning on the ground while planted (visible at about 2 cm).
2. The body turning faster than the feet can step round: a planted leg wrung round in its hip, then a catch-up step.
   Real 180 standing turns let each step turn the foot only about 60-95 degrees, with 3-4 steps [S1], [S2].
3. All parts turning at once ("en bloc"): real turns go eyes, head, chest, hips, feet, with the head 0.05-0.15 s
   ahead of the pelvis and up to 15-28 degrees ahead at peak [S1], [S2]. Very slow real turns do become more en bloc
   [S1], so a slow body turning as one piece is not wrong in itself.
4. Swing foot taking the straight line and cutting through the stance leg in an arc or spin turn [S6].
5. No braking: real walking 180 turns slow down (about 1.1 m/s at 90 down to 1.0 at 180 in [S3]) by shortening
   steps, and the inside leg's steps shorten most.
6. Changing direction in mid-air or between steps; real foot placement and trunk rotation both start within one step,
   timed to a foot strike [S4], [S7].

## 5. What this suggests for the game (for the Game Director to judge; nothing here is decided)

- A walking about-face could be done as: brake on one step, then 2-3 turning steps of about 60-90 degrees each, head
  leading by about 0.1 s and up to about 20 degrees, then walk off. Total about 1.5-2.5 s at normal pace.
- A 420 degrees/s body yaw is far above the peak **head** speed in quick standing 180 turns (about 270 degrees/s in
  [S1]); the pelvis is slower still. If the body yaws ahead of the feet, the 62 degree hip-twist limit will often be
  hit and the body waits, which may read as "wind up, then snap".
- Choose step turn or spin turn by which foot is in front (Hase & Stein), and bow the swing foot round the stance leg.
