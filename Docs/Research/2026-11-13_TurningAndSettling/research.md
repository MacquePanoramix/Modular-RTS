## 1. For Luis, first (half a page)

**Turning while walking.** People do not swing the whole body round at one speed. The eyes jump to where they want to
go, the head follows at once, then the chest, then the hips, and the next foot is put down already pointing the new
way. A gentle turn is spread over a few steps. A sharp 90 degree corner is mostly done in **one** step: the step
before brakes and leans, the turning step swings the hips round by 60 to 90 degrees, the step after pushes off the
new way. The whole corner takes about 1.5 s and about 4 steps. People slow down for it by taking shorter steps, and
the inside leg's steps are shortest. A planted leg twists in its hip by only about 30 to 35 degrees in all during
such a turn; before it would twist further, the person steps.

**Turning right round.** Walking round a post, about 5 steps and a bit over 2 s. From standing, a quick 180 takes
3 steps in under 2 s: head first, a twentieth of a second later the chest, then the hips, then the first foot lifts,
then the second; each foot turns about 75 to 95 degrees when it is set down. The head gets 15 to 28 degrees ahead of
the hips. Only very slow turns go "all in one piece". A person who must suddenly go the other way often steps
toward the new way before the body has finished turning.

**Settling after a shove.** Nobody has measured the steps people take back into their comfortable stance after a
caught push. What is measured: the arms fly up before the catching foot lifts; the body's sway is back near rest
within 2 to 3 s; the heart is up for a few seconds and back within 10 s. People do return to their own stance
(about hip width, toes a little out), but how and when is not known from studies.

**Shifting while standing.** Real people standing freely make a small out-and-back fidget about once a minute and
truly move their weight to a new place only every 3 to 5 minutes, at very irregular times, more often the longer
they stand and the more uncomfortable they get. A shift begins with a brief press the "wrong" way, onto the leg
that is about to be unloaded, for about a quarter of a second.

**Questions for you (not decisions):**
1. Should the walk's body yaw be tied to the steps (most of the turn during one stance, prepared by the step
   before), instead of a steady rate up to 420 degrees a second? People's hips average about 60 to 80 degrees a
   second over a whole walking turn.
2. Should a planted leg step round sooner, at about 30 to 35 degrees of hip twist instead of 62? (The human number
   comes from healthy older adults; no young-adult figure was found.)
3. Should the head lead the hips by a visible but small time (about 0.05 to 0.12 s in quick standing turns) and by up
   to 15 to 28 degrees, more for bigger turns?
4. For a sharp turn, should the choice of a wide side step or a pivot with the legs crossing depend on which foot is
   down, and should the swinging foot bow round the standing leg instead of cutting through it?
5. After a caught shove, should the miner, unhurried, step back into its own stance once its sway has died down
   (2 to 3 s), and should that be one closing step or more after a bigger push? This would be our own choice:
   no study measures it.
6. Should standing shifts be much rarer than the current rule (a shift every 9 to 26 s, from the October page): a
   fidget about once a minute, a true shift every few minutes, growing with time and tiredness? Whether that reads
   as alive at the game's camera distance is for your eyes.

## 2. Frame by frame, in words

Each step names its source. Times are for healthy young adults unless said. Where I join studies together, it says
"synthesis"; such a sequence was not filmed as one in any study.

### 2a. A 90 degree turn while walking (synthesis)

Walking at about 1.2 m/s, about 113 steps a minute, so one step is about 0.53 s (Madrid 2024; Yamaguchi 2017).

1. **About 1 s / about 1.1 m before the corner.** The eyes jump to the end of the new path; the head starts turning
   at the same moment (Hollands 2002, 30-60 degree turns; Grasso 1998, 1 s lead at 90 degree corners, also in the
   dark; Sreenivasa 2008, 1.1 m for turns up to 135). One other study saw the head start only about 50 ms before the
   first foot contact of the turn, the eyes about 400 ms before (Baker 2020, 10 young adults, 90 degree left turn):
   see "Disagreements".
2. **The approach stride (one stride before).** Shorter and slower than straight walking; the foot brakes more and
   pushes less; the body leans back and to the side (Strike & Taylor 2009; Glaister 2008; Xu 2004).
3. **The double support before the turning step.** Most of the turning (yaw) momentum is made here, with both feet
   down (Tillman 2023).
4. **The turning step (apex).** On the outer leg, the sideways push into the turn is about 2 times (planned) to 4
   times (late-cued) that of a straight step (Tillman 2023). By this foot's push-off the pelvis has turned about 60
   degrees (step turn) or about 90 degrees (spin turn) (Yamaguchi 2017). The stance hip twists through about 31
   (step) to 35 (spin) degrees in all (Steingrebe 2024, older adults). The centre of mass swings round a radius of
   only 0.2 to 0.35 m (Yamaguchi 2017). The head points further into the turn than the trunk (Sreenivasa 2008) and,
   on a tight corner, rolls in by up to 8 degrees (Imai 2001).
5. **The next foot lands already pointing into the new way** (Courtine & Schieppati 2003, curved paths).
6. **The exit step.** Brakes less, pushes more; speed is still below normal one stride later (Glaister 2008; Strike
   & Taylor 2009).
7. **Whole turn** (pelvis turning faster than 30 degrees/s): 1.46 s, 3.75 steps, 1.13 m/s round a post (Madrid
   2024). Mean pelvis yaw over that time: about 62 degrees/s (my division, 90/1.46).

Step turn or spin turn: turning away from the foot that is down = the free foot steps wide toward the new way (step
turn, wider and steadier); turning toward it = pivot on it, swing leg crossing in front (spin turn, narrow, the
least steady moment) (Hase & Stein 1999; He 2018). Young adults chose step turns in 73% of late-cued 90 degree turns
(Tillman 2023).

### 2b. A 180 degree turn while walking (synthesis; the weakest of the five)

1. **Before.** The head begins turning; for larger turns its lead over the trunk is larger and peaks later in the
   turn (Sreenivasa 2008, 45-180 degrees round a post). Speed falls more the larger the angle (same).
2. **Braking.** The start of a sudden turn looks like a quick stop (Hase & Stein 1999; the angle is not given in the
   abstract).
3. **The turn round a post.** 2.22 s, 5.1 steps, 0.99 m/s, cadence unchanged at about 111 steps/min; steps shortened,
   inside leg 0.45 m, outside leg 0.58 m (Madrid 2024, 10 young adults, path free). Mean pelvis yaw about 81
   degrees/s (my division).
4. **A sharp about-face** (reversing on the spot while walking): no opened study gives it step by step for young
   adults. Animation practice: people start stepping toward the new way before the body has turned (Johansen 2009,
   from Muybridge's photographs).

### 2c. A 180 degree turn from standing (one lab, two studies)

Young adults turning as fast as they felt safe (Khobkhun 2022, n = 20); times after the cue:

| time (s) | what happens |
|---|---|
| 0.48 | head starts turning |
| 0.53 | chest (thorax) starts |
| 0.58 | pelvis starts |
| 0.62 | first (leading) foot starts |
| 0.86 | second (trailing) foot starts |
| about 2.3 | last foot placed; 3.25 steps in all, each foot turned about 95 degrees |

Peak head yaw speed 173 degrees/s; peak head-ahead-of-pelvis 15-28 degrees (see the table caveat in section 3).
Paced to finish in 1.5 s (Khobkhun 2021, n = 21): head 0.52, pelvis 0.57, leading foot 0.69, trailing foot 0.89 s;
2.95 steps over 1.72 s; peak head yaw 272 degrees/s; head 22 degrees ahead of the pelvis. Slower turns use more
steps (4.3 in 3 s). Below a head speed of about 100 degrees/s head and pelvis turn nearly as one piece (2021,
Discussion). A 90 degree standing turn: 1.95 steps in 0.95 s (2022).

### 2d. Settling after a caught shove (synthesis; mostly unmeasured)

1. **0 s, the push.** The arms rise before the catching foot lifts, higher for bigger pushes (Jeon 2022, standing on
   a belt that jerks forward; 9 young, 10 older).
2. **The catch.** Young adults catch most large pulls with one step; older adults with several, shorter, lower,
   earlier steps (Luchies 1994, backward waist pulls, women). In older adults pushed sideways, several steps began
   with a crossover in 56%, and only multi-step catches had leg collisions (Batcir 2022).
3. **0-3 s.** Sway dies down: centre-of-pressure travel 20.7 cm in the first second, 6.1 in the next, 4.8 in the
   third, against 4.3 cm/s at 4-5 s (Kimura 2024: a voluntary 10 cm step down onto one leg, not a push).
4. **1-2 s.** Heart rate up about 7-10 beats a minute from about 82; blood pressure up at 4-5 s, normal by 6 s; both
   normal before the next jolt 8-10 s later (Siedlecki 2022). Breathing not measured.
5. **Afterwards.** The step back into the comfortable stance: **no study found that counts or times it.**

### 2e. A weight shift while standing (start measured; the rest inferred)

1. Side to side with the feet beside each other, weight moves between the legs by the hips loading one leg and
   unloading the other; front-back sway is at the ankles (Winter 1996).
2. Before a step from standing, the pressure under the feet first moves toward the leg that will be unloaded, by
   about 4-5 cm for about 240-250 ms, throwing the body over the other leg; the whole preparation lasts about 0.5 s
   (Laudani 2021, 10 young adults). A plain weight shift likely starts the same way: **inferred, not measured.**
3. A "weight shift" in long-standing studies is a change of more than 10% of body weight on one leg held at least
   7.5 s; a "fidget" is the same change gone out and back within 0.75-7.5 s (Rekant 2019).
4. The order of hips, knees, trunk and head inside one shift: **not found.**

## 4. What makes such movement look wrong (to measure in the game)

Each is a measurable quantity for the twitches and twisted legs the Director saw.

1. **A planted foot that slides or spins.** Viewers notice about 2 cm of foot slide (Prazak 2011, not opened; its
   abstract figure is "<21mm in most conditions" by a search summary). "Even small amounts of footskate can destroy
   the realism" (Johansen 2009, checked). Measure: per frame, the travel and yaw of each planted foot.
2. **The hip wrung round over a planted foot.** People use about 31-35 degrees of stance-hip twist in a 90 turn
   (older adults); the game allows 62. Measure: stance-hip yaw per frame, and how long it sits above 35 degrees.
3. **The body turning faster than the feet.** Whole walking turns: pelvis about 60-80 degrees/s on average (Madrid
   2024, derived). The fastest head in a 1.5 s standing about-face peaks at about 270 degrees/s (Khobkhun 2021); the
   pelvis is slower. The game's body yaw goes up to 420 degrees/s. Measure: body and pelvis yaw speed per frame
   during turns; count frames above 270 degrees/s.
4. **All of one piece ("en bloc").** Healthy quick turns go eyes, head, chest, hips, feet, with the head 0.05-0.12 s
   and 15-28 degrees ahead of the pelvis; only slow turns (head under about 100 degrees/s) are nearly en bloc
   (Khobkhun 2021). En bloc turning is the mark of Parkinson's disease and fear of falling (R1, from search
   listings; not re-checked).
5. **The swinging foot cutting through the standing leg.** A foot flown in a straight line between footprints on an
   arc makes the outer foot pass through the other leg; real feet follow the arc (Johansen 2009, section 7.3.4,
   checked). Measure: the smallest distance between the two shins during swing.
6. **Changing direction in mid-air.** Valve switched clips only while a foot was on the ground, to avoid the body
   seeming to change direction in mid-air; big departures from a clip's own direction looked unnatural (van den
   Heuvel 2021, checked). Measure: body-yaw change during frames with no foot planted.
7. **No braking.** Real turns slow by shortening steps, the inside leg most (Madrid 2024). Measure: step length per
   leg through the turn.
8. **Twitches.** Frame-to-frame jumps in a joint angle. Animators call blends under 0.2 s a pop (Jirik 2026,
   practice). Measure: per-frame angular speed and acceleration of each joint; flag spikes, every rendered frame.
9. **Clockwork idles.** Real shifts come at irregular times (SD as large as the mean, Duarte & Zatsiorsky 1999) and
   never perfectly symmetric (Jirik 2026, practice). Measure: the spread of intervals between shifts.

## 5. Disagreements, gaps, what is unclear

- **How far ahead the head leads.** About 1 s before a 90 corner (Grasso 1998); about 200 ms ahead of body yaw on a
  curve (Courtine 2003); about 50 ms before the first foot contact of the turn, eyes about 400 ms (Baker 2020); 0.05-
  0.12 s before the pelvis in standing turns (Khobkhun 2021). They measure from different moments and tasks; they
  agree on the order, not on one number. **Unclear.**
- **How people slow down for a turn.** By cadence (Yamaguchi 2017, which notes force plates may have limited step
  length) or by step length with cadence kept (Madrid 2024). **Unclear.**
- **Step turn or spin turn.** Young adults lean to step turns (73% late-cued, Tillman 2023; over 80% at 60 deg,
  Patla 1991 cited in He 2018, not opened); older adults spin (Akram 2010). Pre-planned turns about 50:50 in
  Tillman's wording, which is ambiguous.
- **Hip twist numbers come only from older adults** (63 y). No young-adult stance-hip twist in turns was found.
- **"Step size" in standing turns** (74-95 deg per step) times the number of steps adds to more than the turn, so it
  is likely each foot's own yaw when placed, not its share of the turn.
- **Khobkhun 2022's table labels are misprinted**; head-pelvis separation is 16/23/28 or 20/24/22 deg.
- **No step-by-step study of a sharp walking about-face** in young adults; only turns round a post (Madrid 2024).
- **How many degrees a planted foot spins on its ball or heel** in a walking turn: not found.
- **What follows a caught push** (closing steps, which foot, timing, trunk re-centring, the look): not found.
- **The order of parts inside a standing weight shift:** not found.
- **Whether tiredness enlarges sway:** mixed (Kozinc 2021: after fatiguing runs, women's one-leg sway speed fell,
  men's unchanged; checked). **Unclear.**
- **Animators versus measurement:** animators loop weight shifts every 4-8 s; people shift every 3-5 minutes and
  fidget about once a minute.
