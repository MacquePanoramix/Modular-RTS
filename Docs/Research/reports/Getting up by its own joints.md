# Feedback, not poses, raises the miner

Every body that reliably gets up off the floor using only its own joints does one thing the miner's current riser does not. **It corrects itself from what the body is actually doing at the moment its support changes, and it makes the moment when nothing holds it as short as possible.** The miner instead plays key poses found by search, on a schedule. The research literature names that family as fragile, and its own authors found get-ups to be its hardest case. The miner's chaotic results from one try to the next are the expected symptom, and it fails exactly where robots have their single risky moment: letting go with the hands from a hands-and-feet stance. No shipped game gets a character up by joint torques alone. Games play an animation, pull the body with forces from outside it, or push the head up as a joke. So this project is working at a research frontier, and the best evidence comes from three places: a small robot that rose 100 times in 100, learned policies on real humanoid robots that rise 78–100% of the time, and one offline method on a 40 kg simulated body that rose 10, 10 and 9 times in 10 from hard starting positions. The paths most promising here, in order: **(1) designed balance feedback at the hand release**, combined with toddler strategies that suit Small, whose head is a quarter of its weight, the same share as an 18-month-old's; **(2) changing the existing search so it rewards robustness**, by scoring over varied bodies and then growing extra branches for the starts that fail; **(3) a learned controller trained inside Unity**, aimed first at the failing transfer alone and attempted only after Unity's simulation speed has been measured. Planning ahead in real time (online look-ahead) is probably too expensive in Unity's physics engine to run for every miner. One miner's planner would need its body simulated 50–100 times faster than real time, and the project's bench manages roughly 20–35 times. Some methods use help that exists only during training, such as extra strength or a lifting force. Whether that is acceptable is a question for the game director, not for engineering.

## No shipped game raises a body by its own joints alone

The survey first settles that shipped games offer nothing to copy. Where the evidence is firm, the rise itself is either animation or an outside force. Overgrowth's released source code lets the ragdoll lie until a one-second recovery timer runs out and the body has nearly stopped moving. It then plays an authored "stand from front" or "stand from back" clip, blended from the ragdoll's last pose ([Overgrowth source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/WolfireGames/overgrowth/main/Data/Scripts/aschar.as)).

Unity's PuppetMaster is the best-known active-ragdoll tool. It moves its "muscles" with joint motors, but it also holds each bone toward an animated target with "pins", which its documentation describes as "simple AddForce". Its get-up state blends that target into a face-down or face-up get-up clip while the pins regain strength ([PuppetMaster docs](http://root-motion.com/puppetmasterdox/html/page5.html); [BehaviourPuppet](http://root-motion.com/puppetmasterdox/html/page10.html)). Pins set to zero are the only setting the miner's rule allows, and nothing in the tool is built to make the rise succeed that way.

Unreal Engine's physical animation offers drives in world space, which are forces from outside the body. Its documented get-up is a snapshot of the ragdoll pose blended into a get-up montage ([Epic: PhysicalAnimationData](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/python-api/class/PhysicalAnimationData?application_version=5.3); [Epic: Pose Snapshot](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/unreal-engine/animation-pose-snapshot-in-unreal-engine)). Rockstar describes Euphoria as enhancing "physics-based reactions" within a larger animation system ([Wccftech](https://wccftech.com/rockstar-euphoria-evolved-rdr2/amp/)). In GTA V's multiplayer, players see ragdolled characters stand up on their own when a ragdoll timer expires ([cfx.re forum](https://forum.cfx.re/t/add-native-to-hinder-players-from-getting-back-up-again-automatically-when-ragdolled/5215234)). That Euphoria's get-ups are animated is an inference, but no source contradicts it. Tencent's Wobbledoll balances with a learned controller, then "blend[s] to kinematic get-up animation" once the body settles ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/wobbledoll-developing-an-advanced-full-body-ragdoll-system/)).

Comedy games push the body up. Totally Accurate Battle Simulator applies **an upward force to the torso and head, scaled by the head's height above the floor**, whenever a unit is "able to stand". It also adds forces to the legs and feet ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/design/how-landfall-games-finds-the-fun-in-physics-engines)). Other developers guess that Gang Beasts holds its characters up in a similar way ([Unity forum](https://discussions.unity.com/t/how-to-create-a-physic-based-ragdoll-character-controller-like-gangbeasts/595806)), but no developer of Gang Beasts or Human: Fall Flat has confirmed it.

Games that keep the body honest pay a visible price. Exanima's developer concedes that its physics-driven motion "will realistically always feel tricky and look a bit silly for a new player" ([Bare Mettle forums](https://www.baremettle.com/forums/index.php?threads/exanima-0-8.12202/)). Half Sword players report trouble simply standing up ([Steam](https://steamcommunity.com/app/2397300/discussions/0/3949154954318457031)). Sumotori Dreams' self-righting dummies were received as comedy ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumotori_Dreams)). In Toribash and QWOP the joints alone move the body, but the player is the one controlling them ([QWOP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QWOP)).

Two studio data points stand out. Ubisoft La Forge's DReCon characters "move and balance entirely using their own strength", with joint motors capped at 200 N·m. Yet the authors write that they "have not yet developed good methods of making a character stand up from a fall in a natural looking way" ([DReCon paper](https://static-wordpress.ubisoft.com/montreal.ubisoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/13214229/DReCon.pdf)). Embark Studios described the learned locomotion of ARC Raiders' robots at GDC 2026. On another project, they said, they had added a non-physical "magic force" on a robot's head to keep it upright, and then **penalised its use during training so that the motion stayed natural** ([4Gamer report](https://www.4gamer.net/games/609/G060942/20260312064/)). That is the only production precedent found for the director's stance, which treats outside help as a crutch to be removed. Verdict on this family: nothing in it leaves the body solely responsible. If the project reaches its goal, it will be ahead of shipped practice, not catching up with it.

## The current riser fails where open-loop methods always fail

Two terms first. A **PD drive** is a joint motor that acts like a spring and a damper. It pulls the joint toward a target angle, and its force is capped at the joint's strength. **Open-loop** control fixes the plan in advance: the drives are told which pose to aim for at which moment, whatever the body is actually doing. **Closed-loop** control, or **feedback**, computes each next command from the body's measured state.

The miner's riser is mostly open-loop, with three feedback patches: weight is shifted off a limb before it lifts, planted contacts are held in place, and the centre of mass is balanced over the supports. Its key poses came from a sampling search. That puts it in the family of SAMCON (2010), which samples changes to the PD targets every 0.1 s and keeps the best path. SAMCON's authors call their controls "open-loop in nature" and "not robust with respect to external perturbations". They found the **get-up their hardest case: reconstructions failed two times in three at their standard sample counts, and one time in four with roughly three times as many samples** ([SAMCON 2010](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2010-TOG-sampControl/2010-TOG-samcon.pdf)). The improved 2015 version was still open-loop, and it warned that sampling noise shows up as "vibration and jerkiness" ([Improved SAMCON](http://cfcs.pku.edu.cn/libinliu/Samcon2/Samcon2_EG.pdf)). The project's own records show the same narrowness. Searched ways stood Small up 7 times in 8 at the place where they were searched, but **0 times in 18** when the three miners were laid at six other places on the same path ([Validation.md](../../Validation.md)).

Two traps in the current design are documented elsewhere. The first is driving the body to a known pose and then running a fixed rising strategy. The authors of the strongest robustness study on getting up tried exactly that. They report that it "does not work with our controllers. The character never exactly reaches the designated target pose and the resulting errors typically make the controllers fail" ([Al Borno et al. 2017](https://www.cs.utoronto.ca/~mazen/DOA/DOA.pdf)).

The second trap is handing control from one controller to the next without the two agreeing on where the body should be. Faloutsos and colleagues' "composable controllers" (2001) require each controller to declare three things: a pre-condition (where it is able to start), a post-condition (where it promises to leave the body), and a running monitor that detects failure. Their riser from the back stops "on its feet with its center of mass within the support polygon", which means in a crouch, and a separate controller then raises it. Their standing balancer refuses to start unless the centre of mass is moving slower than 0.3 m/s, accelerating less than 0.1 m/s², and the posture is within 0.1 radian of its own ([Faloutsos et al. 2001](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2001-siggraph-composable.pdf)). The miner's second failure point, "as it is first raised", looks like exactly this kind of hand-over: the body arrives somewhere the standing controller cannot handle. That is my reading; no source has studied the miner.

Robots explain the first failure point. A small humanoid robot that rose 100 times in 100 lab tests had exactly one dynamic phase, in which it straightened its arms to tip its weight over the front edges of its feet. Its designers found a narrow window for that phase: "If they are too small, the COM projection will not leave the support polygon. Too high values cause the backward motion to overshoot" ([Stückler et al. 2006](https://www.ais.uni-bonn.de/behnke/papers/IAS9.pdf)). That is the miner's failure precisely: it tips back onto its heels, or forward, as the hands lift. B-Human's NAO robot team found the timed version of the same problem: "the next keyframe starting too early causes the robot to fall back" ([B-Human 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02129v1)). A Japanese study of a robot that rolled and rose dynamically found that success and failure were separated not by the overall trajectory but by "energy input timings" at a few critical moments ([Kuniyoshi et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.7210%2Fjrsj.23.706)). **Large swings from try to try are what an open-loop plan produces when it meets a knife-edge.**

Part of the chaos may come from how the simulation is set up. PhysX promises identical results only for "the exact same scene", with actors inserted in the same order, and adding actors, even ones that never touch, can change the outcome ([PhysX docs](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.4.1/docs/RigidBodyDynamics.html)). It also carries contact information from one step to the next that cannot be saved and restored ([Isaac Sim PhysX limitations](https://docs.isaacsim.omniverse.nvidia.com/5.1.0/physics/physics_resources.html)). Unity offers an "Enhanced Determinism" setting that makes a scene's result independent of the other actors in it, at some cost in speed ([Unity physics settings](https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.0/Documentation/Manual/class-PhysicsManager.html)). Separating setup noise from a fragile controller is cheap. Either way, the cure for fragility is feedback: a controller that works only when a run is replayed exactly is not robust.

## Designed feedback: shrink the gap, then let go on a momentum test

Three terms recur in this section. The **centre of mass** is the single point where the body's weight acts as a whole. With Small's heavy head, it sits well forward of the hips. The **support polygon** is the area on the ground enclosed by everything touching it. The **capture point** is the spot the body's weight is heading for. It is computed from where the centre of mass is now, plus its speed multiplied by a factor set by its height. If that spot is inside the boots, the body can come to rest over them. If it is outside, the body will tip unless something else holds it. Robots call this quantity the "divergent component of motion".

Hand-designed controllers and robot control systems agree on five mechanisms that together handle the exact moment where the miner falls.

**Shrink the gap while still supported.** The 100-in-100 robot owed its wide margin to "the shortness of the dynamic phases, which is achieved by moving the COM projection as close as possible to the foot polygon in the preliminary static phases" ([Stückler et al. 2006](https://www.ais.uni-bonn.de/behnke/papers/IAS9.pdf)). AIST's HRP-2P, 154 cm and 58 kg, rose through phases that were stable at every moment plus one short controlled transition from kneeling to crouching. It needed that one dynamic step because its knees and soles could not be on the floor at the same time (same source). Kenwright's planner walks the hands back toward the feet. It lifts one hand and moves the weight toward the feet over the triangle formed by the two feet and the other hand. It then replants the free hand nearer, and repeats until the centre of mass is over the feet. Only then are both hands released ([Kenwright 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.05056)). Jones's prone riser places the arms "directly under the current shoulder position", one at a time, before planting a foot ([Jones 2011](https://users.cs.utah.edu/~benjones/ubc_2011_fall_jones_benjamin.pdf)).

**Transfer the load gradually, and confirm it before lifting.** Jones ramped each limb's share of the balance from full to zero over the half-second before it lifted, and raised that limb's target 4 cm over the same half-second. He also paused the sequence "until it is safe to proceed… stance limbs are in contact with the ground, and swing limbs are not supporting weight" ([Jones 2011](https://users.cs.utah.edu/~benjones/ubc_2011_fall_jones_benjamin.pdf)). Coros and colleagues start walking only once the centre of mass has moved 40% of the way to the new standing foot ([Coros et al. 2010](http://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2010-TOG-gbwc/paper.pdf)). Both rules are **state-triggered**: the next stage begins when the body reaches a condition, not when a clock says so.

**Push through the limbs with "virtual forces", and decide how much each contact pushes.** A virtual force is the set of joint torques along a planted arm or leg that make it push the body as if a hand from outside were pushing. The push itself is real: it is the limb's own joints pressing against the floor, so nothing outside the body acts on it. SIMBICON leaves the standing hip free so that "the resulting torques be physically realizable without the use of external torques" ([Yin et al. 2007](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2007-siggraph-simbicon.pdf)). Coros and colleagues add **gravity compensation**, the torque each joint needs to hold up the limbs beyond it, so the posture springs can stay soft. They credit those low gains for "natural, highly compliant motion", and their 70 kg walker survived 600 N shoves lasting 0.1 s ([Coros et al. 2010](http://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2010-TOG-gbwc/paper.pdf)). Torque-controlled robots go one step further with **contact-force distribution**. Each control step, they work out the push needed to restore the centre of mass and the trunk. They share that push among the touching hands and feet within friction limits, using as little force as possible, and convert the shares into joint torques ([Ott, Roa and Hirzinger 2011, abstract](https://elib.dlr.de/73492)). For the miner, this would turn "weight off the hands" from a side-effect of pose changes into a number the controller sets and drives to zero. Unity's ArticulationBody provides the pieces this needs: a Jacobian, which converts a push at a hand or foot into joint torques, and the joint torques gravity demands ([Unity ArticulationBody](https://docs.unity3d.com/ScriptReference/ArticulationBody.html)).

**Let go on a momentum test, not a clock.** Zambella and colleagues (2023) got DLR's 79 kg TORO robot up from a position with its hands on a support. It pushed with its arms and let go once the capture-point quantity "is already on a trajectory that allows the robot to recover the upward position". Pushing past that point made it "fall on its back" ([Zambella et al. 2023](https://repositum.tuwien.at/bitstream/20.500.12708/189191/1/Zambella-2023-IEEE%20Robotics%20and%20Automation%20Letters-vor.pdf)). The authors call earlier release timings "heuristic" because they were set by trial. Two caveats limit the transfer to the miner. TORO's hands rested on a chair or wall about 1 m high, not on the floor. And **its 50 N per arm narrowed the range of positions it could rise from**, which is the robot version of the miner's short arms (same source).

**Balance in the crouch before rising; watch for failure and retry.** Faloutsos's separate crouch stage handles the first. Coros's balance push runs up from each standing ankle, and its ankle part is switched off when the foot is not flat ([Coros et al. 2010](http://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2010-TOG-gbwc/paper.pdf)). B-Human builds keyframed stand-ups with a centre-of-mass balancer, torso checks, waits until the torso has tipped over, and on failure a soft fall followed by a retry. Its success rates were **76.5–98.1% depending on the year and the playing surface, against about 55–94% for other teams** ([B-Human 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02129v1)). Faloutsos's failure monitor exists so that "another more appropriate controller can take over" early ([Faloutsos et al. 2001](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2001-siggraph-composable.pdf)). These retry patterns match the director's preference for extra tries over help.

Verdict on this family. **It keeps the body fully responsible**, because virtual forces and contact-force sharing are joint torques reacting against the ground, provided they are implemented that way and never as a force on the hips. The best robustness numbers come from robots: 100 in 100, and above 90% with checks and retries. In animation, Jones's two-dimensional prone riser succeeded in at least 80% of 16 disturbed runs after optimisation, on slopes from −12° to +17° and with a torso 1.45 times larger ([Jones 2011](https://users.cs.utah.edu/~benjones/ubc_2011_fall_jones_benjamin.pdf)). No three-dimensional rise from hands-and-feet onto the feet has a published success rate, and Kenwright reports none at all. Slow, supported rises read as deliberate. Rushed ones do not: Jones's rises optimised down to 1.2 s were "quite unnatural and include violent collisions with the ground" (same source). The per-frame cost for 13 parts is negligible (my estimate). Building it is medium effort, because it extends the miner's existing centre-of-mass balance, planted holds and standing controller.

The risks are specific. Kenwright's static method needs the upper body to be between one and two times the length of the lower body ([Kenwright 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.05056)). That rule hints that body proportions decide whether a fully supported release exists at all. If no hand placement brings the weight over the boots, a short push plus the momentum test is unavoidable. Virtual forces assume a planted base: Jones saw feet slip, and they may fight Unity's stiff PD drives. A centre-of-mass-over-feet test can be "arbitrarily bad" on uneven ground ([Bretl and Lall 2008](https://lall.stanford.edu/papers/bretl_eqmcut_ieee_tro_projection_2008_08_01_01/pubdata/entry.pdf)), and the meadow is not flat. Finally, the director has ruled out responses that switch at a threshold. A gate decides *when* the hands lift; the load sharing around it must ramp smoothly so that nothing jumps.

## Small is built like an 18-month-old, and the bear stance is its natural road

The project's weighed figures matter here. **Small weighs 40.7 kg, of which its head is 10.0 kg, a quarter. Long's head is 6.5 of 58.5 kg and Round's is 9.8 of 87.6 kg, about 11% each** ([ThePhysicalBody.md](../../Design/ThePhysicalBody.md)). Child crash-test dummy design data put the head at 25.5% of body mass at 12 months, 24.4% at 18 months, 21% at three years, 11.3% at ten, and 5.8% for an adult man ([dummy design table](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/anthropomorphic-test-devices-and-injury-risk-assessments/)). By head share, **Small is an 18-month-old, while Long and Round are ten-year-olds**. Toddler evidence therefore applies above all to Small.

Toddlers rise in a recognisable order. Marsala and VanSant filmed 60 children aged 15 to 47 months. The youngest most often used the patterns predicted to come first in development, and the study found two leg patterns not described before ([Marsala and VanSant 1998](https://academic.oup.com/ptj/article-lookup/doi/10.1093/ptj/78.2.149)). A secondary review names them "pike" and "pike-jump to squat" (read only as a search excerpt). Clinicians report that toddlers first come up through a bear stance (hands and feet down, bottom up) or a squat, and reach the half-kneel only later, as balance and strength grow ([Starfish Therapies](https://starfishtherapies.com/2020/07/floor-to-stand)). Rising from a squat is harder than lowering into one: it arrives around 14 months and needs more strength, a large head makes the balance harder, and toddlers overshoot and undershoot while learning ([Kinedu](https://blog.kinedu.com/the-not-so-simple-action-of-standing-up-part-3)). Children aged 3 to 6 who rose in the more hands-free way were stronger and steadier than those who pushed with their hands: grip 6.9 against 4.4 kg, and one-leg standing 24.4 against 14.2 s ([Kuwabara et al. 2013](https://file.scirp.org/Html/31326.html)). Small's failed half-kneel and its bear route therefore fit how real children develop (my inference). The bear route is also mainstream among adults: **a third of independent older adults rise by a hands-and-feet push-up, which takes 6.3 s on average against 4.0 s for the half-kneel** ([Stutzenberger and Whited 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12452367/)). Even young adults are uneven about it. Most often one hand leaves the floor before the other ([Pacific University thesis](https://commons.pacificu.edu/works/publication-dissertation/8evd5-4x010)), and only a quarter use the fully symmetrical pattern ([VanSant 1988](https://academic.oup.com/ptj/article-lookup/doi/10.1093/ptj/68.2.185)).

A rough strength check suggests that the miners fall because of where their weight is, not for lack of leg strength. Yoshioka's simulations of slow rising from a 0.40 m chair found that **the peak hip torque plus the peak knee torque, per leg, must exceed about 1.53 N·m per kilogram of body weight**. Leaning the trunk forward moves that work from the knee to the hip ([Yoshioka et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1929086/)). Scaling that figure by height (my assumption) gives about 51 N·m per leg for Small, 96 for Long and 120 for Round. The miners' caps are knee plus hip of 217 N·m per leg for Small, 505 for Long and 551 for Round ([TheBodysOwn.md](../../Design/TheBodysOwn.md)). That is four to five times the demand of a slow chair rise. A deep squat from the floor demands more, but no study gives a figure for it. Strength is therefore probably not the limit, which agrees with the robot evidence that the moment of release decides everything.

Bodies that are weak, rather than heavy-headed, show which strategy levers work. In Gowers' manoeuvre, children with weak hips walk their hands back toward their feet, keep the trunk folded, widen their stance, and rise onto their toes, which keeps the line of force in front of the knee ([Chang and Mubarak 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3369091/)). Gowers' original 1879 description has **one hand pushing on the knee while the other pushes on the ground** ([LITFL](https://litfl.com/gowers-sign/)). A push of the miner's hand on its own thigh is produced by its shoulder and elbow and reacts within the body, so it breaks no rule. With short thighs, the knee is within reach in a deep squat.

Other levers come from squatting. Heels rising in a squat mean the weight is too far forward, and bringing the arms forward acts as a counterweight ([Yoga International](https://yogainternational.com/article/view/why-your-heels-might-not-reach-the-ground-in-malasana-squat)). Moving the centre of pressure from 44% to 54% of foot length raised the ankle's torque by about 30% and lowered the knee's by 6% ([JSSM 2022](https://www.jssm.org/jssm-21-341.xml-Fulltext)). Older adults rising from the floor bent their ankles about 30° each way ([Burton et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9958992/)).

The strategy levers available, all of them the body's own, are these. Release one hand before the other. Move the near hand onto the knee or thigh. Keep the trunk and head low and forward until the weight is over the boots, then let the head lead the rise. Place the feet further forward under the hips with a deeper ankle bend. Widen the stance. Lift the heels slightly. Reach forward with the arms as the hands leave the floor. The body levers change how the miner looks, so they are the director's to choose: a smaller head share (closer to the 17–21% of a three- to six-year-old), more ankle range, a longer heel or a broader boot sole. This family is not a controller on its own. What it does is widen the window the controller has to hit, at no cost when the game runs.

## Planning ahead online needs a simulator a hundred times faster than the game

**Model-predictive control (MPC)** means this: every few hundredths of a second, the controller simulates many short candidate futures, called rollouts, of the next half-second in a hidden copy of the physics. It picks the best one, carries out only its beginning, and repeats. DeepMind's MuJoCo MPC shows how far this can go. A humanoid "can be initialised on the floor and quickly stands in a manner that is robust to large disturbances", and each planning update takes 1–20 ms. The simplest planner tries only 10 candidate futures of 23 steps each, scored on six terms with nothing about style ([MuJoCo MPC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.00541); [settings table](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.00541)). The catch is the simulator. MuJoCo runs that humanoid **"4000 times faster than realtime on a single CPU thread"**, and the authors warn that "one can only control what can be simulated much faster than real-time" and that planning is "myopic", because it cannot see past its short horizon (same source). The repository calls itself "not production-quality software" ([GitHub](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mujoco_mpc)).

Earlier work shows the costs in torque and robustness. Tassa's 2012 planner got a humanoid up from arbitrary poses at 7 times slower than real time, needing 4,000 physics evaluations per step ([Tassa et al. 2012](https://02465material.pages.compute.dtu.dk/02465public/_assets/tassa2012.pdf)). Critics note that it "gets up with a single bounce, implying that large torques are at play", and **failed to get up when torques were capped at ±300 N·m** ([Al Borno et al. 2017](https://www.cs.utoronto.ca/~mazen/DOA/DOA.pdf)). Hämäläinen's 2014 sampling planner could "improvise a get up strategy" ([abstract](https://history.siggraph.org/?p=103531)). An independent reimplementation, however, steered random lying states toward a get-up controller successfully only **9, 4 and 2 times in 10** on progressively harder starts, and failed from some face-down and face-up postures ([Al Borno et al. 2017](https://www.cs.utoronto.ca/~mazen/DOA/DOA.pdf)). The author's page lists "no Unity examples yet" ([Hämäläinen](https://users.aalto.fi/~hamalap5/publications.html)).

Unity can do this mechanically. It supports separate physics scenes stepped by script ([Unity multi-scene physics](https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.0/Documentation/Manual/physics-multi-scene.html)), and reading and writing the joint positions, velocities and drive targets of a whole articulation ([Unity ArticulationBody](https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.0/Documentation/ScriptReference/ArticulationBody.html)). But a copy only approximates the live body, because contact state cannot be copied. PhysX articulations are also "more expensive than regular joints" ([PhysX best practices](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.4.1/docs/BestPractices.html)), and no published benchmark of ArticulationBody speed was found.

A budget estimate makes the problem concrete. Ten rollouts of 25 steps, replanned 10–20 times a second, come to **2,500–5,000 physics steps per second of play: 50 to 100 times real time for one miner** (my arithmetic from the published settings). The project's own bench gives an approximate comparison. These are figures from this session's runs, not a benchmark: Small alone, a 0.02 s physics step, a game frame between every physics step, batch mode, nothing drawn. The roll search ran 240 tries in about 41 s, most ending about 3.5 s into play, which is **roughly 20 times real time**. The roll-up search ran 360 tries in about 138 s, most ending 10–15 s in, which is **roughly 30–35 times real time**. So **one miner's planner would need two to five times everything the bench now simulates**, before the game itself runs. Stripping the game frame and running copies side by side would raise the bench's figure, but by how much is unmeasured.

Verdict. The body stays fully responsible: only drive targets are commanded, and the planning copies are invisible. Robustness is good against shoves but degrades on hard lying positions. Without effort and smoothness terms the motion is opportunistic, with bounces. The cost is the blocker, and building it is medium-to-high effort. The one use worth testing is narrow: seed the planner with the existing poses and let it correct only the 0.3–0.6 s around the hand release. Even that only makes sense if Unity's measured speed allows it.

## Offline search and learning buy robustness with days of computation

### Trees and feedback grown around searched motions

A controller's **domain of attraction** is the set of starting states from which it succeeds. Al Borno, van de Panne and Fiume (2017) grow that domain deliberately. Each controller is a schedule of PD targets, optimised by search. The method then picks a start that fails, finds by optimisation a short connection from it to some state already known to succeed, and adds that branch to a tree. Their body is the closest published match to Small: **40.5 kg, 1.61 m, with torque limits of ±150 N·m**. The result was **10, 10 and 9 successes in 10, against 9, 4 and 2 for online sampling**. The authors found that "a small tree, e.g., 80 nodes, is sufficient to obtain a robust rising controller that is invariant with respect to the facing direction". Full trees of 277 to 1,377 branches took 1–3 days on a desktop computer, and choosing a branch at run time by a few short look-ahead runs averaged real time on one core. The authors concede the motions "do not always look natural", and that their clock-keyed controllers lack robustness ([Al Borno et al. 2017](https://www.cs.utoronto.ca/~mazen/DOA/DOA.pdf)).

The SAMCON group itself turned open-loop searched motion into feedback in 2016. Every 0.1 s slice of a motion gets a simple linear correction computed from a handful of body measurements. The corrections are learned by fitting them to many searched solutions, and the process is repeated, because "none of the motion skills can acquire robust feedback policies with only one iteration"; up to 20 were used. **Face-down and face-up get-ups then ran automatically after falls, in real time.** This cost about one to two hours of computing per second of motion on 20 cores, and no get-up success rate was reported ([Liu et al. 2016](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2016-TOG-controlGraphs/2016-TOG-controlGraphs.pdf)). The run-time cost is one small multiplication every 0.1 s.

Contact-invariant optimisation discovers get-ups from nothing more than a "stand" goal, finding different strategies from the back and from the stomach. But it does so in simplified physics: all the mass sits in the trunk, the limbs are "infinitely strong", and early on, contact forces may act "from a distance" ([Mordatch et al. 2012](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~zoran/behavior-discovery.pdf)). Its output is a plan, not a controller. Its successor made plans robust enough for a real Darwin-OP robot by optimising across an ensemble of slightly different models of the robot, and reported get-up success "similarly high" to walking's 80–95% (read only through a search summary) ([Mordatch et al. 2015](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~todorov/papers/MordatchICRA15.pdf)). That points to the **cheapest robustness lever for the project's existing search: score every candidate over several slightly different bodies, not over one.**

Verdict. The tree method and the learned slice corrections both run in full physics on joint drives, so the body stays responsible. Contact-invariant optimisation uses non-physical help, but only inside the optimiser, and its plan must still be carried out by a real controller. This family has the best robustness evidence from animation research and costs little at run time. It is mixed on naturalness, costs days of offline computing (more in Unity than in MuJoCo), and takes high effort to build. Its specific risks are clock-keyed branches when the body falls behind schedule, and success labels that flip under PhysX's non-determinism.

### Learned policies: strong numbers on robots, GPU-sized bills

**Reinforcement learning** trains a **policy**, a small neural network that reads the body's state many times a second and outputs the joints' target angles, through millions of simulated tries. Tao, Wilson, Gou and van de Panne (2022) used no motion capture on a **38.3 kg** character. They trained it in three stages: first with a body stronger than normal, then made weaker step by step, then slowed down. **Training directly at 40–60% strength "failed to discover any get-up mode."** The authors judged 40–60% strength to look more natural than above 70%. The slowed motions could be paused at many points without falling. Each stage took about 24 hours on a 32-core desktop with a graphics card, and the paper reports no success rate ([Tao et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307)). A reimplementation on a Unitree G1 robot reached 92.6% from the back and 99.0% from the front, but with "unstable, jumping motions" ([HumanUP](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12152)).

The 2025 robot results set the bar. HumanUP first discovers a get-up with simplified collision shapes, then trains a policy to follow an 8-times slowed copy of it from about 20,000 randomised lying poses. It reached **95.3% from the back and 92.1% from the front in simulation, and 78.3% on a real G1 against 41.7% for the manufacturer's controller**. Without randomised starts, the simulated rates fell to 65.4% and 30.3%. Training took about 5 billion simulation steps on a graphics card ([HumanUP](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12152)).

HoST reached 94.2–99.8% in simulation and 20 of 20 on a real G1. During training, however, it used **an upward assist force on the robot's base, starting at 200 N and reduced to zero; without it, success was 0%**. Without its smoothness regularisation, real success fell to 11 of 20, with oscillation ([HoST](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08378)). HiFAR rose 20 of 20 times from back and front on a real Booster T1, but its simulated success from the back fell to 60% at 75% of full torque ([HiFAR](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.20061v1)). FRASA trained only 5 joints, front-to-back and mirrored left to right, in 13–37 minutes on a desktop ([FRASA](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.08655v3)). Robotis OP3 robots learned to reach goal poses taken from their scripted get-up, and the learned version rose in 0.93 s against 2.52 s for the script ([Haarnoja et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.13653)).

Animation research shows the bill. DeepMimic's face-down and face-up get-up clips each took 49–66 million samples on a 13-part, 45 kg humanoid ([DeepMimic](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1804.02717)). AMP policies took 100–300 million samples, or 30–140 hours on 16 cores ([AMP](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.02180)). ASE recovered from all 500 test falls, many of them stumbles rather than full get-ups, after about 10 billion samples and 10 days on a graphics card ([ASE](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.01906)). PHC recovered 95% of the time within 5 s and states "We do not use any external forces". By contrast, a tracker that relied on outside "residual" forces fell from 96.4% to 62.6% on test motions without them, which rules that kind of tracker out for this project ([PHC](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.06456)).

Inside Unity, the ML-Agents 4.x package provides sensors for ArticulationBody characters ([ML-Agents API](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.ml-agents@4.0/api/Unity.MLAgents.Sensors.html)). It runs trained networks through Unity's Inference Engine, where the processor is faster than the graphics card for small models ([Inference Engine](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.ml-agents@4.0/manual/Inference-Engine.html)). It also trains in several Unity instances at once ([Training ML-Agents](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.ml-agents@4.0/manual/Training-ML-Agents.html)). But no documented get-up trained in Unity was found, and no figure for how fast ArticulationBody characters train. Two risks carry over from the project's own experience. Learning systems game what they are asked: Disney's jumping robot character "tends to cheat by keeping the toes on the ground" ([Disney 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.05204v1)), just as the project's pose search gamed its scores. And a changed body needs retraining ([MorphCon](https://mrl.snu.ac.kr/publications/ProjectMorphCon/MorphCon.html), search excerpt).

A cheaper start exists. DReCon's policy learns only small corrections on top of fixed animation targets ([Ubisoft La Forge](https://www.ubisoft.com/en-us/studio/laforge/news/VjEIwquaIyEZZSw5RZI0V/drecon-datadriven-responsive-control-of-physicsbased-characters)). That is the same shape as correcting the miner's existing searched poses.

Verdict. During play the body stays fully responsible, because the policy only sets joint targets. The training aids (extra strength, an assist force, simplified shapes) are for the director to judge. Tao's result warns that training at realistic strength from the start may find nothing at all. This family has the strongest numbers anywhere. Its naturalness depends on smoothness terms and slowing down. A small network costs little during play (my estimate is under a quarter-million multiplications per decision). It takes high effort to build, and its training budget in Unity is unknown. A rough sum from the bench's approximate 30 times real time: a DeepMimic-sized budget of about 50 million decisions, at 50 decisions a second, is a million seconds of play, or about nine hours of one bench. That is affordable, and parallel instances would shorten it. A HumanUP-sized budget of billions of steps is not.

Three questions in this table are the director's, not the engineer's. The first is whether help that exists **only during training or offline search** is acceptable when the finished body never receives it. Tao trained with extra strength that was later taken away; HoST used a lifting force reduced to nothing; contact-invariant optimisation uses forces "from a distance" ([Tao](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307); [HoST](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08378); [Mordatch 2012](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~zoran/behavior-discovery.pdf)). The same question would apply to running the project's own pose search with stronger joints and then tightening them. Embark's practice, an assist used and then trained away, is the nearest industry precedent ([4Gamer](https://www.4gamer.net/games/609/G060942/20260312064/)). The second is whether body changes such as a smaller head share, more ankle range or a longer heel are open. The third is whether a hand on the knee and a one-hand-first release suit the miners' character.

## Three paths worth taking, each with a cheap first test

The ranking weighs four things: evidence that a method works across varied falls, fit with the director's rules, cost when the game runs, and how much of what the project already has can be reused. The three paths compound rather than compete. Jones's thesis is exactly path 1 tuned by path 2: a designed controller whose settings were searched for success across 16 disturbed runs ([Jones 2011](https://users.cs.utah.edu/~benjones/ubc_2011_fall_jones_benjamin.pdf)).

### First: make the hand release a controlled moment

This path keeps the body fully responsible, costs nothing measurable when the game runs, reuses the riser's planted holds, centre-of-mass balance and standing controller, and aims straight at both failure points. The robot recipe has five parts. Bring the weight as close to the boots as possible while still supported: hands walked back one at a time, feet further forward, trunk and head low. Ramp the hands' share of the load down to zero rather than switching it off. Lift the hands only when the capture point lies inside the boots with a margin and the hands carry almost nothing. Balance in the crouch before rising. Retry from hands-and-feet when a monitor sees the fall coming. On top of that sit the toddler and Gowers strategies: one hand leaves first and the near hand pushes on the knee ([Stückler](https://www.ais.uni-bonn.de/behnke/papers/IAS9.pdf); [Jones](https://users.cs.utah.edu/~benjones/ubc_2011_fall_jones_benjamin.pdf); [Zambella](https://repositum.tuwien.at/bitstream/20.500.12708/189191/1/Zambella-2023-IEEE%20Robotics%20and%20Automation%20Letters-vor.pdf); [Chang and Mubarak](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3369091/)). The main uncertainty is whether short arms allow a supported release at all. The first test answers that.

The first experiment is **"where is the weight when the hands let go?"**, and it needs no new controller. Instrument the bench to record, at the instant each hand leaves the floor in the 36 face-down tries, three things: where the whole-body centre of mass and the capture point lie relative to the boots (toe links included), how much weight the hands still carry, and how fast the body is moving. Compare the tries that stood with the tries that fell, on all three miners and at several places, as the project already does. If the fallers let go with the capture point outside the boots or with load still on the hands, add the gate. The hands stay down, and the hips sit back or a hand walks nearer, until the condition holds. Then count the 36 again. If even the standers let go with the weight outside the boots, a supported release does not exist for this body, and the next step is a short push released on the momentum test. A second small change belongs to the same test: hand over to the standing controller only when Faloutsos-style conditions hold (weight over the soles, moving slowly), and let it hold the crouch height briefly before raising ([Faloutsos et al. 2001](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2001-siggraph-composable.pdf)). This is a day or two of work. Success looks like a face-down count well above 8 of 36, with less spread between seeds.

### Second: make the search reward robustness, then grow branches

The project's search finds poses that work for one lucky set of conditions. The literature's fix comes in two steps. The first is cheap: score each candidate across an ensemble of slightly different bodies and conditions, which made plans hold up on a real robot ([Mordatch et al. 2015](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~todorov/papers/MordatchICRA15.pdf)). The second is expensive: grow a tree of branches from starts that fail, which gave 10, 10 and 9 in 10 on a body of Small's weight ([Al Borno et al. 2017](https://www.cs.utoronto.ca/~mazen/DOA/DOA.pdf)). This keeps the body fully responsible, adds almost nothing at run time, and uses the bench the project already has. Its cost is offline computing time, and the naturalness of what it finds must still be judged in pictures.

The first experiment is **"how much of the chaos is the setup?"**. Turn on Enhanced Determinism and build each try freshly, in a fixed order ([Unity physics settings](https://docs.unity3d.com/6000.0/Documentation/Manual/class-PhysicsManager.html)). Run the current best way's 36-try count twice. If two identical runs now give identical counts, part of the spread was the setup. Then count the same way under five slightly changed bodies: joint strength ±10%, ground friction ±20%, head mass ±10%, and a little timing jitter. That ensemble count is the honest measure of robustness, and from then on the search should climb on it. A branch test can follow within days. Collect the body's state from every try at the moment just before the hands lift, sort those states into two or three groups (for example, weight further forward against further back), and search a separate continuation for each group, choosing at run time by the nearest group. That is a three-branch tree in the spirit of Al Borno's 80-branch one.

### Third: learn the transfer in Unity, once Unity's speed is known

Learned policies have the strongest numbers anywhere, and during play only joint targets are commanded. But every published success used far more simulation than Unity has been shown to deliver, and none used a body shaped like the miners' ([HumanUP](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12152); [HoST](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08378)). The way in is narrow and cheap, following FRASA's reduced problem ([FRASA](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.08655v3)) and DReCon's corrections layered on fixed targets ([Ubisoft La Forge](https://www.ubisoft.com/en-us/studio/laforge/news/VjEIwquaIyEZZSw5RZI0V/drecon-datadriven-responsive-control-of-physicsbased-characters)).

The first experiment has two halves. The first half is a measurement that also settles whether online planning could ever be affordable. The bench already runs one Small at roughly 20–35 times real time with a game frame between steps. **Time script-stepped physics without that frame, for 1, 8, 16 and 32 copies of Small in one scene.** If the copies together reach a few hundred times real time, a night of training covers a CPU-era budget, and the second half is worth doing. Train an ML-Agents policy for the hands-and-feet-to-crouch transfer only, for Small only, mirrored left to right. Its output is a small correction to the existing way's joint targets, its starts are drawn from the bench's hands-and-feet states with random variation, and its reward is reaching a still crouch over the boots, with penalties for jerky or wasteful effort. Run it at the real strength caps, with no training help, so that the director's question does not arise yet. One night of training should show whether it beats the gate from path 1, and the pictures should be checked for jitter. If it learns nothing at realistic strength, Tao's experience predicts exactly that, and the strong-to-weak schedule becomes the question to bring to the director ([Tao et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307)).

Some things should be left aside for now. The first is anything from shipped games, since all of it either animates the rise or pulls the body. The second is the derivative-based planner (iLQG), which bounced its humanoid up and failed under realistic torque caps ([Al Borno et al. 2017](https://www.cs.utoronto.ca/~mazen/DOA/DOA.pdf)). The third is contact-invariant optimisation used as a controller. The fourth is any tracker that relies on residual forces. The fifth is online planning for every miner, at least until the measurement above says otherwise. Whole-body momentum control, which produces arms that windmill for balance ([Macchietto et al. 2009](https://www.cs.ucr.edu/~cshelton/papers/MacZorShe09.html)), is a later refinement once the miner already stands.

## Conclusion

The miner's two failure points are one failure seen twice: a hand-over between stages that do not agree on where the weight must be. When the hands lift, the support stage leaves the body where the feet cannot hold it. When the standing controller takes over, it receives a body outside what it can handle. That shifts the work. The task is no longer finding better poses; it is making each hand-over a checked, measured transfer, so that the pose search only has to deliver the body into a region where feedback can take over. It also means the riser's success count is a measurement of something specific and should be treated as one. A cheap recording of where the capture point lies at the instant of release will show whether the miner needs a supported release, a short push, or a different body. The bench's approximate speed already rules out look-ahead planning for every miner. One clean timing of physics copies side by side will settle how far learning can go.

The second takeaway is that Small's difficulty is not a defect to engineer away. With a head that carries a quarter of its weight, it is shaped like an 18-month-old, and 18-month-olds rise exactly as it is trying to: slowly, through a hands-and-feet stance, overshooting and undershooting as they learn. Real children manage the moment it fails at by keeping a hand on the floor or on a knee. If the project gets a body of these proportions up by its own joints, reliably and in a way that reads like a toddler, it will have done something no shipped game has done and that a well-resourced studio lab has said it could not yet do naturally.
