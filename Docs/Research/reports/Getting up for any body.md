# Design the rise, learn only the corrections

Wonder Gather should get its player-built bodies off the floor with a **procedural controller that generalises by construction**, not with a neural policy trained across bodies. Every choice it makes should be computed from the body's own geometry, masses, joint ranges and strength caps: which stance it can hold, where each hand and foot goes, when a limb may let go, and how long each stage takes. Soft, gravity-compensated joint drives working inside anatomical limits should carry those choices out, and the controller should be tuned once, during development, over a sampled population of bodies, never per body. No published method yet gets arbitrary bodies up naturally by their own joints, so this is the best-supported direction, not a demonstrated solution. The case rests on four findings. Learning across bodies works for walking but not yet for rising: the only cross-body get-up study reached 17–42% success on six of seven unseen robots; published budgets run from 10^8 to 10^10 samples against the project's roughly one million a night; and no learned get-up has been shown to look natural to viewers. Online planning adapts to any body by construction, but it needs on the order of a thousand simulated seconds per real second for each fallen unit, and its own authors call the result stiff and noisy. Controllers built from the body's own numbers have already done three relevant things: carried characters across edited proportions with nothing retuned but mass-scaled gains; animated arbitrary player-made creatures in a shipped game at 0.2 ms each; and, when a planned rise was carried out in physics, scored 6.29 against motion capture's 6.92 for naturalness. Human floor-rise science supplies requirement (3) directly. People choose their way up by capacity: the ratio of required to available joint moment, their joint range, and where their mass sits. A controller that computes those numbers makes proportions and strength visible by design. Finally, most of the twitching and broken-looking limbs the director saw are faults of the body and the drawing, not of the method. The getting-up body's joints have no limits at all, its light boots spin past 150 rad/s, and the one-frame flicks came from the rig adapter, the cause this research ranked first. Those must be mended before any method can look natural. A single learned corrector, trained once across sampled bodies, stays an option for the dynamic transitions, once a measurement of Unity's simulation speed shows it is affordable.

## Learning across bodies works for walking, not yet for rising

The strongest argument for a learned controller is that one network can now drive bodies it never saw. GenLoco (2022) trained a quadruped policy over bodies scaled 0.8–1.2×, with link masses at 0.5–1.5× and motor strength at 0.8–1.2×. It then ran zero-shot on ten unseen simulated robots and three real ones. Its gait held until a body was about **twice the largest training size**, after which performance "drops sharply" ([GenLoco](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2209.05309)). XHugWBC (ICML 2026) did the same for humanoids built from one template, stretched 0.8–1.5× with torque limits at 0.7–1.0. It reached **about 85% of a specialist's return on 12 unseen humanoids** and drove seven real robots ([XHugWBC](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.05791)). LocoFormer's long-memory transformer scored 0.96 on unseen robots against a dedicated expert's 0.99 ([LocoFormer](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.23745v1)). Humanoids, however, are the weakest class in every multi-body study. URMA found "no meaningful zero-shot transfer" to humanoids ([URMA](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.06366)), and in a 2025 scaling study across 1,012 generated robots the real H1 humanoid did worse than the Go2 quadruped ([embodiment scaling laws](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.05753)). Every one of these results is locomotion, and none stands up from the ground. Their body ranges are also modest, roughly ±20–50% per link, nowhere near the spread from toddler-like to giant that a character creator invites.

Getting up across bodies has one direct study, and it is sobering. Spraggett (December 2025) trained a single policy for seven small humanoids of 0.48–0.81 m and 2.8–7.8 kg. On bodies held out of training it succeeded **17–42% of the time for six of the seven**, and 72% for the seventh. **Top-heavy and long-armed robots failed most.** A training set made only of extreme robots reached at most 16%. Gains stopped after four or five training bodies, and adding bodies sometimes hurt the others ([Spraggett 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.12230v1)). Even that problem was made easy: five pitch joints commanded symmetrically, at 1.5 hours of training per policy. Wonder Gather's hardest current body is exactly the class that failed, since Small carries 10.0 of its 40.7 kg in its head ([ThePhysicalBody](../../Design/ThePhysicalBody.md)), and player-built long-limbed bodies would join it. The author's main lesson applies to any tuning across bodies, learned or not: **continuous, overlapping coverage of size, mass and limb ratio matters more than diversity**.

Character animation has one paper built around varying body shape. Won and Lee's MorphCon (2019) gave one controller body-shape parameters, with each segment scaled between 0.35× and 1.8×, and controlled new characters "immediately without any re-training process". It needed 2–4 days and 50–100 million samples, it walks only, and 20.7% of 300 random lower-body bipeds still fell within 30 s. The authors concede that the network's "representation power ... is often insufficient for learning a full spectrum of body shape variations" ([MorphCon](https://mrl.snu.ac.kr/publications/ProjectMorphCon/MorphCon.pdf)). Physics trackers that accept body shapes, such as PHC, publish results only for the mean shape ([PHC](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.06456)), and the shapes they learn from are those of adult motion-capture subjects. **No published controller produces natural, body-specific get-ups across a continuous space of proportions and strengths.**

Strength shows both the promise of learning and its trap. Robotics varies strength narrowly, 0.90–1.10× in RMA's training and 0.9–1.1× in HoST ([RMA](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2107.04034); [HoST](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08378)), precisely so that the policy behaves the same at every strength. That is the opposite of requirement (3). Where weakness is a wide, known input and the caps actually bind, style changes visibly. Generative GaitNet produced foot drop, a dropping pelvis with trunk lean (Trendelenburg gait) and crouch gaits from per-muscle weakness in one network, though for walking only ([Generative GaitNet](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2201.12044)). Tao and colleagues found strong characters' get-ups "excessively dynamic", while final strengths of 40–60% of a human default looked "more natural". Yet training at 40–60% from the start "failed to discover any get-up mode", so strength had to be annealed from strong to weak and ended up fixed inside each policy rather than given as an input ([Tao et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307)). HiFAR's success from the back fell to 60% at 75% of full torque ([HiFAR](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.20061v1)). No study shows one policy that is told its strength and chooses visibly different, natural get-ups across strengths.

No learned get-up has been shown to look natural to viewers. Robot get-ups are judged by jitter and smoothness numbers, and they reach good ones only through heavy machinery. HoST bounds how far a joint target may move per step; without that bound its smoothness cost rose from 2.90 to 9.52, and without its Lipschitz-style (L2C2) regularisation the real robot rose 11 times in 20 instead of 20 ([HoST](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08378)). HumanUP had to track its own get-up slowed eightfold and still shows "unnatural hand raising". Its authors measured Tao's method on the same robot at **ten to thirty times HumanUP's action jitter** ([HumanUP](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.12152v2)). The nearest viewer study, of a learned safe-falling controller, found that 25 participants preferred motion capture on six of eight aspects ([Boban and Sužnjević 2025](https://scitepress.org/PublishedPapers/2025/132592)). An SCA 2023 paper argues that learned policies are typically "too stiff, too strong, too smart" compared with people ([Xie et al. 2023](https://about.roblox.com/publications/too-stiff-too-strong-too-smart-evaluating-fundamental-problems-with-motion-control-policies)). The get-ups that do look human borrow a human motion prior. Frezzato's ablation is the clearest. Without a pose space learned from motion capture the agent failed to rise. With unlimited torques it rose but looked "very robotic". Realistic torques were "very important" ([Frezzato thesis](https://espace.etsmtl.ca/id/eprint/3082/1/FREZZATO_Anthony.pdf)). But a prior learned on one body pulls every body toward that body's movement. Cosmo is a robot whose head is 16% of its mass, and it hopped and stepped at high frequency until data recorded on its own body was added ([Cosmo](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.05581v1)). That pull is the opposite of letting proportions show. The richest get-up motion data are also closed to a commercial game: LaFAN1 is non-commercial with no derivatives, and AMASS forbids training networks for commercial use ([LaFAN1](https://github.com/ubisoft/ubisoft-laforge-animation-dataset); [AMASS licence](https://amass.is.tue.mpg.de/license.html)).

Cost settles the question for now. Cross-body results used **10^8 to 10^10 samples**, and one used 2×10^12. URMA took 1.6 billion steps over 2.5 days on 20 cores and a desktop GPU, and PHC about 10 billion in a week on an A100 ([URMA](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.06366); [PHC](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2305.06456)). The project's own teaching ran about **900,000 steps through one night** ([CURRENT_STATE](../../CURRENT_STATE.md)), roughly 30 steps a second, at which rate a billion steps would take about a year. GPU simulators change the arithmetic: MuJoCo Playground trains humanoid walking in under 30 minutes on two RTX 4090s ([MuJoCo Playground](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08844)). On a Windows laptop, though, the paths are narrow. JAX has no native Windows GPU support, and its WSL2 route is "experimental" ([JAX installation](https://docs.jax.dev/en/latest/installation.html)). MuJoCo Warp lists native Windows support but needs CUDA 12.4 or later ([mujoco-warp](https://pypi.org/project/mujoco-warp/3.5.0/)). Isaac Sim's minimum is an RTX 4080 with 16 GB ([Isaac Sim requirements](https://docs.isaacsim.omniverse.nvidia.com/latest/installation/requirements.html)). A policy trained in another engine must then run in Unity's PhysX, a transfer between engines for which no quantitative study exists. One Unity project porting MuJoCo humanoids to PhysX still listed its PhysX version as "being translated" ([modular-agents](https://github.com/Balint-H/modular-agents)). Running a trained network is the cheap part. A network of HoST's size needs on the order of a million operations per decision, about 1.5–3 GFLOP/s for 100 units at 30 Hz (an estimate), and Unity's Inference Engine runs models this small fastest on the CPU ([ML-Agents inference](https://docs.unity3d.com/Packages/com.unity.ml-agents%404.0/manual/Inference-Engine.html)).

The production record agrees. No shipped game was found in which characters get up by a learned physics controller. ARC Raiders' learned robot locomotion needed adversarial motion priors before it looked good enough, allows "magic forces" against falling, took "a team of five to ten people working for years", and was nearly cut ([80.lv interview](https://80.lv/articles/inside-the-magic-of-machine-learning-that-powers-enemy-ai-in-arc-raiders)). Tencent's Wobbledoll balances with a learned policy, then blends into a kinematic get-up animation ([Wobbledoll](https://80.lv/articles/wobbledoll-developing-an-advanced-full-body-ragdoll-system/)). Ubisoft's DReCon authors wrote that they "have not yet developed good methods of making a character stand up from a fall in a natural looking way" ([DReCon](https://static-wordpress.ubisoft.com/montreal.ubisoft.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/11/13214229/DReCon.pdf)). **Learning remains the best tool for a narrow job**, correcting a transition that a designed controller handles poorly. On present evidence it cannot carry any player body up naturally on its own.

## Controllers built from the body's own numbers generalise by construction

The alternative to teaching a network every body is a controller that never needs to be told which body it has, because it computes everything from the body in front of it. Three families do this. Planners simulate the actual body as it moves; optimisers compute a plan for each body design; designed controllers express their targets and feedback in the body's own units. All three adapt by construction. They differ in cost and in look.

Online planning is the purest form. Hämäläinen and colleagues' sampling planner (2014) improvised multi-stage get-ups from a simple balance cost, with no motion data: "planting a hand on the ground, leaning on the hand to allow moving a foot closer, and finally shifting weight on the foot to rise up" ([SMC 2014](https://mediatech.aalto.fi/publications/graphics/OnlineSMC/preprint.pdf)). The same paper shows the bill: 25 candidate futures of 2 s each per frame, at about 20 frames a second on a 12-thread desktop and 6–10 on a 2012 laptop. That is **roughly a thousand simulated character-seconds per real second for one body** (arithmetic from those settings). The 2015 successor ran "only barely real-time" on six cores ([C-PBP 2015](https://mediatech.aalto.fi/publications/graphics/C-PBP/preprint.pdf)). The authors call the motion "sometimes stiff" with "unnecessary joint contortions", and "slightly stiff and noisy". They also found that lowering the sampling noise cost robustness unless the sample count grew. They rejected iterative solvers, naming "ODE, Bullet Physics or PhysX" engines, as "not stable enough for active characters except at very small time steps". They also needed a fully repeatable simulator, because otherwise "the sampler sometimes forgets a chosen control strategy" ([SMC 2014](https://mediatech.aalto.fi/publications/graphics/OnlineSMC/preprint.pdf)). Newer GPU samplers walk real quadrupeds with 2,048 samples per step on an RTX 4090, but none of them gets up from the ground ([DIAL-MPC](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2409.15610)). Plain sampling (MPPI) failed to stabilise a real H1 humanoid ([FS-MPC](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.19443)). The one head-to-head test is unflattering. A reimplementation of the 2014 planner steered lying bodies into a get-up 9, 4 and 2 times in 10 on progressively harder starts, against 10, 10 and 9 for an offline tree of controllers ([Al Borno et al. 2017](https://www.cs.utoronto.ca/~mazen/DOA/DOA.pdf)). The project's bench simulates one body at roughly 20–35 times real time ([October report](<Getting up by its own joints.md>)), 30–50 times short of a single planner's need even with only one unit down. A MuJoCo copy of each body could serve as a faster planning model. MuJoCo claims 4,000 times real time for a humanoid on one thread ([MJPC](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.00541)), and its Unity plug-in runs MuJoCo inside the Unity editor and at run time ([MuJoCo Unity plug-in](https://mujoco.readthedocs.io/en/stable/unity.html)). But the plug-in's documentation states neither its maintenance, nor its Unity 6 support, nor whether it works in standalone builds, and the planning would still be noisy. Online planning belongs, at most, in a narrow corrector or an offline discovery tool.

Optimising a plan for each body design is cheaper per unit and shows proportions most clearly. Contact-invariant optimisation (2012) discovered get-ups from nothing but a standing goal. For "a biped with a wide torso and quadruped with short legs" it found "strategies appropriate for each morphology", at **2–10 minutes per clip** on 2012 hardware ([CIO](https://homes.cs.washington.edu/~zoran/behavior-discovery.pdf)). Its limbs are treated as "infinitely strong", though, so it cannot show strength, and its output is only a plan that a separate controller must carry out. Lin and Huang (2012) did carry such a plan out. A posture planner followed by a physics filter produced visibly different rises for bodies with shoulders twice as wide, arms 1.6 times as long, and an upper body 2.2 times as long, at about 17 s of computation per rise on a Core 2 Duo desktop. Forty viewers rated it **6.29 out of 10 against 6.92 for motion capture**, the only viewer study of a physics-based get-up found, although its final squat-to-stand was motion capture ([Lin and Huang 2012](https://people.cs.nycu.edu.tw/~wclin/rising/rising_tvcj2012.pdf)). Optimisation on the scale of seconds exists only for simplified models with fixed contact patterns, such as Disney's walking robot designs, solved in "a few seconds" according to a press release ([CMU](https://www.ri.cmu.edu/?p=111864)). Whether a few seconds of computation per body design counts as the "training" the director ruled out is his call ([October 30 message](../../Correspondence/2026-10-30_THE_GETTING_UP_FOR_THE_WHOLE_GAME.md)).

Designed controllers are the cheapest way to generalise, and the evidence that they do comes from walking. Coros, Beaudoin and van de Panne (2010) drove four characters of different proportions and let users "interactively edit the dimensions and proportions of a character and immediately see the resulting motions", with "no character-specific or style-specific parameter tuning". **The only setting that depended on the body was joint stiffness, scaled by mass.** Without that scaling, "large characters ... knees begin to buckle under their weight". The system ran in real time on one core of a 2010 machine, and users with no animation experience built varied characters in 5–10 minutes ([Coros et al. 2010](http://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2010-TOG-gbwc/paper.pdf)). It generalises because every target is expressed in the body's own geometry, with limb poses solved within each limb and foot placement taken from an inverted pendulum sized to the body. Every correction reads the body's own state, and gravity compensation keeps gains low enough for "natural, highly compliant motion". One finding needs care: torque limits had "no visible impact" on walking, but rising is limited by strength, so in a riser **strength must enter the plan, not only the drives**. Jones (2011) built rising controllers from the same parts: pose tracking, virtual forces pushed through the planted limbs, and a support graph that "can pause phase advancement until it is safe to proceed". Each limb's share of the load was ramped from full to zero over the half-second before it lifted. After optimisation, his two-dimensional prone riser succeeded in **at least 80% of 16 disturbed runs**. Continuation then carried it to slopes from −12° to +17° and to a torso 1.45 times larger with 2.1 times the mass. Rushed down to 1.2 s, it became "quite unnatural" with "violent collisions with the ground" ([Jones 2011](https://users.cs.utah.edu/~benjones/ubc_2011_fall_jones_benjamin.pdf)).

The one shipped system built for arbitrary player bodies is Spore's. Its animators never saw the skeletons they animated. Instead they wrote motions as semantics: which parts move (the "graspers", the "feet", the front-most limb), and in which frame relative to the body. A motion could be relative to the rest pose, or scaled to the creature's bounding box. It could be scaled by limb length, giving "large movements on long limbs and small movements on short limbs". Or it could be relative to the ground, so that every body reaches the ground at 1.0. At run time an inverse-kinematics solver fitted each motion to each creature in **about 0.2 ms per 25-part character** on a 1.7 GHz laptop CPU, and **about 90% of animations passed** against an ever-changing library of player creations. When a goal was out of reach, the creature would "reach for them instead of mechanically hyperextend" ([Hecker et al. 2008](https://www.cse.chalmers.se/edu/year/2011/course/TDA361/Advanced%20Computer%20Graphics/027-hecker%20copy.pdf)). Spore is kinematic, and its authors list what it lacked: any sense of volume, any self-collision, and protection from twists at points where the IK solution breaks down. What transfers is the semantics layer, not the animation. A get-up stage written as "hand on the ground under the shoulder" or "foot a fraction of a leg length ahead of the hip" can be solved for any body and handed to a physics controller. Other procedural games do not generalise this way: Rain World writes custom code for each creature ([Unity blog](https://unity.com/blog/exploring-procedural-design-rain-world)), and fully physical bodies such as Wobbledogs are played as comedy.

Robotics adds what animation lacks: a way to let strength and proportions choose the strategy. The **actuation-aware feasible region** (Orsolino et al. 2020) is the set of centre-of-mass positions a stance can hold, given friction and each joint's torque limit. It takes **under 10 ms per four-contact stance**, and it shrinks as load grows, reaching zero at about the moment a joint's torque limit is hit ([feasible region](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1903.07999)). Computed for each candidate stance of a given body, it says which stances that body can hold and with what margin. Weak legs make a deep squat's region vanish; long arms let a hands-and-feet stance's region overlap the feet. It is quasi-static and was validated mainly on a quadruped, so applying it to knees and forearms on the ground is untested. Shape can choose the roll in the same way. Cockroaches right themselves by opening their wings to lower the energy barrier to rolling, after which small leg kicks tip them over ([Othayoth and Li 2021](https://elifesciences.org/articles/60233v1)). The barrier for rolling left, right or onto the front is cheap to compute for any body. Kenwright's rise planner works fully supported only when the upper body is between one and two times the length of the lower body ([Kenwright 2023](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2304.05056)), a reminder that some builds will need a short dynamic push.

Robots also supply the reliability recipe. A kid-size robot rose **100 times in 100**. Its static phases brought its centre of mass "as close as possible to the foot polygon", so the single dynamic push was short and its timing window wide ([Stückler et al. 2006](https://www.ais.uni-bonn.de/behnke/papers/IAS9.pdf)). B-Human's NAO stand-ups reached 76.5–98.1% depending on the year. They did so by waiting until the torso had tipped over, checking the torso, falling softly on failure and retrying ([B-Human 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.02129v1)). DLR's 79 kg TORO let go of its hand supports only when its **capture point**, the place its centre of mass is heading, was already on a course that ends standing. Pushing longer made it fall onto its back, and its weak arms, 50 N each, narrowed the range of starts it could rise from ([Zambella et al. 2023](https://repositum.tuwien.at/bitstream/20.500.12708/189191/1/Zambella-2023-IEEE%20Robotics%20and%20Automation%20Letters-vor.pdf)). That is the robot version of a weak or short-armed player body.

These controllers fail in a known way when they are built carelessly. Driving the body to a planned pose and then running a fixed rise "does not work with our controllers. The character never exactly reaches the designated target pose and the resulting errors typically make the controllers fail" ([Al Borno et al. 2017](https://www.cs.utoronto.ca/~mazen/DOA/DOA.pdf)). The sources agree on four defences. First, each stage declares where it can start, where it promises to leave the body, and how it detects failure early ([Faloutsos et al. 2001](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2001-siggraph-composable.pdf)). Second, targets are recomputed from the measured state at every step. Third, progress is triggered by the body's state, not by a clock. Fourth, a failed stage backs off to the last stable support and tries again. The project's riser already contains three of these feedback pieces: it shifts weight off a limb before lifting it, holds planted contacts in place, and balances the centre of mass over the supports ([October report](<Getting up by its own joints.md>)).

`OwnBody` already uses the right building block. It is made of ArticulationBody links ([OwnBody.cs](../../../Assets/_WonderGather/Scripts/Units/OwnBody.cs)). These are Unity's reduced-coordinate articulations, whose locked axes are "unbreakable and unstretchable by design". Ordinary joints such as ConfigurableJoint, by contrast, satisfy their constraints only if the solver converges, which can produce "stuttery and unrealistic motion" ([Unity manual](https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/6000.0/manual/physics-section/physics-overview/articulations-section/physics-articulations)). The articulation API exposes the two quantities a body-relative controller needs. One is a Jacobian, which converts a desired push at a hand or foot into the joint torques that produce it. The other is the torque gravity demands at each joint ([ArticulationBody API](https://docs.unity3d.com/ScriptReference/ArticulationBody.html)). Sharing the push among four to eight contacts is a small solve, plausibly microseconds per unit (an estimate, not measured). The real costs are physics, since PhysX calls articulations "more expensive than regular joints" ([PhysX best practices](https://nvidia-omniverse.github.io/PhysX/physx/5.4.1/docs/BestPractices.html)), and engineering time.

## The twitches and broken limbs are body and drawing faults first

The director named three faults: "very aggressive twitches with the limbs", worse in dynamic moments; arms bent so they "seem like it's broken"; and a limb that would "twitch like for one millisecond completely out of frame" ([October 30 message](../../Correspondence/2026-10-30_THE_GETTING_UP_FOR_THE_WHOLE_GAME.md)). The research ranked their likely causes before the project's frame-by-frame reading of the game was finished, and the two agree. **The faults sit in the drawing and in the body far more than in the getting-up method** ([frame review](../../Reviews/2026-10-30_GettingUp_EveryFrame.md)). That shapes the choice of method, because every method in this report would inherit them.

The research reasoned that a body whose speed limits hold cannot throw a limb out of frame within one rendered frame, so it ranked the drawing as the likeliest cause of one-frame flicks. Its suspects were stale interpolation state at mode switches, the undefined axis of a from-to rotation when a limb points opposite to its reference ([from-to rotation](https://talk.codea.io/t/quat-fromtorotation-with-opposite-vectors/13912)), quaternion sign flips, interpolation running outside its range, and NaN values. The frame review found the first of these families. The rig adapter rebuilt each drawn limb's twist from where the elbow or knee sat off the limb's line, and that offset has no direction when the limb is straight or folded double. Drawn limbs therefore turned **up to 176 degrees in a frame, 1,036 times in 18 minutes** of play. Drawing each limb from its own physics rotation cut the near-straight cases from 543 to 16. The review also found hands and feet jumping 4–12 cm for one to three frames, about 45 times, mostly the physical whipping described below seen from outside, so a body whose speed limits fail can produce brief flicks of its own ([frame review](../../Reviews/2026-10-30_GettingUp_EveryFrame.md)). Two lessons generalise. First, Unity's interpolation delays a body's drawn pose by one physics step and blends the previous two steps ([Unity interpolation](https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/6000.0/manual/physics-section/physics-overview/rigidbody/rigidbody-interpolation)). At 120 frames a second each 50 Hz physics step spans 2.4 rendered frames, and at 144 about 65% of rendered frames contain no physics step at all (arithmetic). Bugs that live on those frames never appear in a fixed-rate capture, because Unity's capture clock advances by a fixed interval whatever the real frame time ([captureDeltaTime](https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/current/script-reference/unityengine/time/capturedeltatime)). Second, the detector that caught this, comparing every rendered frame with the physics under it, should become permanent. Its checks are: the angle between each drawn bone and its physics part; constant bone lengths; NaN and quaternion-length tests; and the interpolation fraction staying in range. It should run at 30, 60, 120, 144 frames a second and uncapped, with hitches injected, and keep a two-second ring buffer that is dumped whenever a check trips. Smoothness can be tracked with a dimensionless measure such as spectral arc length rather than mean squared jerk ([Balasubramanian et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4674971)). Contact plausibility can be tracked with ground penetration, floating and foot skating ([PhysDiff](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.02500)).

Arms that look broken have a plainer cause. The getting-up body's articulation axes are all set to free motion, so every joint turns freely, held only by motors that give way under the body's weight ([OwnBody.cs](../../../Assets/_WonderGather/Scripts/Units/OwnBody.cs); [frame review](../../Reviews/2026-10-30_GettingUp_EveryFrame.md)). The review measured elbows bent the wrong way in 1–3% of the getting up (once by 70 degrees), knees folded past 150 and up to 180 degrees, hips 89 degrees behind the body, and shoulders turned half round. Adult norms put **elbow extension at about 1–5 degrees past straight, knee flexion at 138–142 degrees, hip extension at 17–18, and forearm rotation at about 77–91 degrees each way** ([CDC joint range of motion study](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/ncbddd/jointrom/index.html)). The cure is unusually cheap here because the body already uses articulations. Each articulation axis can be set to Locked, Limited or Free, with its own lower and upper limit ([ArticulationBody manual](https://docs.unity3d.com/Manual/class-ArticulationBody.html)). Lopsided ranges, such as a shoulder's roughly 170 degrees forward ([CDC](https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/ncbddd/jointrom/index.html)) against about 50 behind ([shoulder model](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2410.05931)), can therefore be set directly, which ConfigurableJoint's symmetric swing cone cannot do. Elbows and knees are already single-axis hinges, so the impossible sideways bend is excluded; what they lack is stops. The sources add two refinements. Some limits are coupled: shoulder abduction ranges from 90 degrees with the arm turned in to 165 degrees with it turned out ([shoulder coupling study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2409.13905)). Coupled limits are best enforced on the targets rather than by the physics. Targets should be clamped inside the anatomical range minus a margin, with the physics limit set slightly wider as a hard backstop. Joint frames should also keep anatomical extremes away from 180 degrees of swing, where PhysX joint parameters have "a nasty singularity" ([PhysX joints guide](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIAGameWorks/PhysX/4.1/physx/documentation/PhysXGuide/Manual/Joints.html)). For player-built bodies the ranges become part of the build: a stiff body gets tighter limits, and the same numbers feed its choice of strategy (next section). Ankle limits should come from loaded, functional range rather than clinical norms. The clinical figure of about 13 degrees of dorsiflexion understates the roughly 30 degrees that older adults actually use when rising from the floor ([Burton et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9958992/)).

The whipping boots are a fault of strength and inertia. Boots spun at **115–154 rad/s** and forearms passed 12 rad/s for about 3,000 frames. In one run a boot's spin spread through the shin to the whole body until it was flung 10^17 metres ([frame review](../../Reviews/2026-10-30_GettingUp_EveryFrame.md)). The review's diagnosis is motors far stronger and quicker than a person's, on light parts, with targets that jump from pose to pose, and that is what the sources predict. A drive capped far above its load reaches any target within a few physics steps and reads as a snap. Strong characters' get-ups are "excessively dynamic" while weaker ones look natural ([Tao et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307)). Torque limits that vary with joint angle and speed, derived from muscle activation bounds, gave "human-like motions comparable to those generated by musculotendon models" ([Jiang et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.13041)). PhysX advises keeping mass ratios between connected bodies below 10 ([PhysX joints guide](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/NVIDIAGameWorks/PhysX/4.1/physx/documentation/PhysXGuide/Manual/Joints.html)), and Unity warns that 10:1 "can become jittery" ([Unity ragdoll stability](https://docs.unity.com/en-us/engine/6000.0/manual/physics-section/physics-overview/ragdoll/ragdoll-stability)); a light boot on a heavy shin approaches that. `OwnBody` already ties each drive's damping, and a ceiling on its stiffness, to the turning inertia of the part it moves, and it gives every link a 20 rad/s speed limit ([OwnBody.cs](../../../Assets/_WonderGather/Scripts/Units/OwnBody.cs)). Yet the recorder measured 154 rad/s, so that setting is not bounding the links, and why is not established. The remedies, in order, are these. Scale strength caps to a human level: DeepMimic's 45 kg humanoid uses 200 N·m at the hip, 150 at the knee, 90 at the ankle, 100 at the shoulder and 60 at the elbow ([DeepMimic humanoid](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/xbpeng/DeepMimic/master/data/characters/humanoid3d.txt)). Make torque fall as joint speed rises, as muscle force does. Cap joint speed per joint; the articulation's joint-speed limit is already in `OwnBody`, but only takes effect when a keeper setting turns it on. Use joint limits as the hard stop. Add a guard that logs any runaway energy or NaN. A rough comparison suggests the miners are strong for natural motion. Young adults use about 42% of their maximal knee moment to rise from a chair ([Hortobágyi et al. 2003](https://biomed.gerontologyjournals.org/cgi/content/abstract/58/5/M453)), a capacity about 2.4 times the demand. October's estimate put Small's hip-plus-knee caps at four to five times a slow chair-rise demand ([October report](<Getting up by its own joints.md>)), though that estimate scaled human figures by height and should be treated as approximate.

The learned corrections now layered on the searched poses add their own twitch, and any future learned corrector would too. Deep reinforcement-learning policies drift toward bang-bang actions, slammed from one extreme to the other, because nothing charges them for changing the action. A policy that used only the extremes of each action matched state-of-the-art results on standard benchmarks ([Seyde et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.02552)). ML-Agents clips continuous outputs to ±3 and divides by 3 ([ML-Agents source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Unity-Technologies/ml-agents/develop/ml-agents/mlagents/trainers/torch_entities/action_model.py)). Under deterministic inference, a mean beyond that range therefore becomes an exact ±1. The research brief described the current layer as a decision every 0.1 s, eased 20% per decision, up to ±35 degrees from the key pose. With those settings a flipped output becomes a 14-degree target step held for five physics steps (arithmetic): a 10 Hz tick into strong drives. The best-measured cures act on the training objective. A Lipschitz penalty cut a humanoid's **action jitter from 42.19 to 3.21** while its task return fell only from 28.87 to 26.03, beating a low-pass filter, which left jitter at 7.86 and return at 24.98 ([LCP](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.11825v2)). CAPS cut a real quadrotor's high-frequency control content by 96% and its motor current by 80%, at the price of a larger tracking error (9.28 against 5.19 deg/s) ([CAPS](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2012.06644)). HoST's per-step bound on target change mattered more for smoothness than its Lipschitz term ([HoST](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.08378)). Filters added after training behave badly. One filter type (FIR) caused total loss of control, and adding a filter during training without showing the policy the filter's state broke learning ([CAPS](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2012.06644)). Any easing must therefore be present in training and visible to the policy. Joint-target actions, which the project already uses, cope best with low decision rates ([Peng and van de Panne 2017](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1611.01055)). The first measurement is cheap: for each joint, the fraction of decisions at the action limit and the mean change per decision, split between calm and dynamic moments.

## Capacity, not age, decides how a human body rises

Requirement (3) asks that the body's build show in how it rises. Human movement science has measured exactly that, and its answer is consistent across children, healthy adults, frail elders, and people with Parkinson's disease, amputation or stroke. **The less capacity a body has relative to the task, the more of it stays on the floor, the more positions it passes through, and the longer it takes.** That makes capacity, not age or a style label, the quantity a procedural model should compute.

The strategies form a ladder. Only 25% of 32 young adults rose by the fully symmetrical sit-up into a squat ([VanSant 1988](https://academic.oup.com/ptj/article-lookup/doi/10.1093/ptj/68.2.185)). **90.6% of 53 community-dwelling adults over 60 rolled partly and rose through an asymmetrical squat** ([Klima et al. 2016](https://digitalcommons.sacredheart.edu/pthms_fac/156)). Among 34 older adults choosing freely, half used a half-kneel (4.04 s), a third a push-up from hands and feet (6.26 s) and a sixth a roll-over (3.68 s) ([Stutzenberger and Whited 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12452367/)). Young adults preferred to sit and then crouch. Frail residents of supported housing preferred "Tuck, Crouch-Kneel, All Fours, and Bearwalk", and 38% of them could not rise without help ([Ulbrich et al. 2000][epmc-vansant]). With Parkinson's disease, 75% used a crouch-kneel and 70% all fours, rising in 14.9 s on average, and 9 of 20 needed a chair ([Klima et al., Europe PMC abstracts][epmc-floor]). Older adults roll into side-lying more and "reposition their hands and feet more often" ([Schwickert et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5017442/)). Below a certain margin the extra positions stop being a style and become the only feasible path: Ulbrich's non-risers reached sitting, kneeling and tuck positions and stopped there.

The capacity variable has good anchors. To rise from a chair, young adults use **42% of their maximal knee moment, older adults 80%** ([Hortobágyi et al. 2003](https://biomed.gerontologyjournals.org/cgi/content/abstract/58/5/M453)), and impaired elders use 97% at the lowest seat they can still manage ([Hughes et al. 1996][epmc-chair]). Rising from a seat without the arms needs a combined knee-extension force of about 46% of body weight ([Bohannon 2009](https://www.bisp-surf.de/Record/PU201001000405)). Relative sit-to-stand power below about 2.1 W/kg in women and 2.6 W/kg in men marks limitation ([Alcazar et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8350203)). The minimum combined peak hip-plus-knee moment for a slow chair rise is about 1.53 N·m/kg per leg ([Yoshioka et al. 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1929086/)). When the hands help in a chair rise, peak hand loads averaged 16–19% of body weight in able older adults ([Anglin and Wyss](https://www.bisp-surf.de/Record/PU199912407641)), and another study measured typically 20–30% of body weight through each arm ([Bristol method study](https://research-information.bris.ac.uk/en/publications/a-method-for-measuring-vertical-forces-applied-to-the-upper-limb-/)). A procedural model can therefore compute, for each candidate stance and joint, a **reserve r = required moment ÷ available moment**, and let it set the style. Below about 0.5: symmetrical and hands-free. From 0.5 to 0.8: asymmetrical, with one hand pushing, through a half-kneel. From 0.8 to 1.0: hands and feet, two-hand pushes, more intermediate positions and pauses. Above 1: a support, a retry, or failure. These bands are a design heuristic built on chair-rise anchors, not measured floor-rise thresholds. A readable output follows from the sitting-rising test, which subtracts a point for every hand, forearm or knee used ([NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/healthmain/can-you-do-simple-sitting-test-predicts-longevity-1c7588565)). A weak or heavy body should visibly use more supports, not just move more slowly. Among 2,002 adults, each extra point on that test went with 21% better survival ([Brito et al. 2014][epmc-floor]), evidence that the support count tracks real capacity.

Joint range decides which paths exist at all. **22.5% of 71 healthy men could not hold a heels-down deep squat**, and ankle dorsiflexion was the strongest factor ([Kasuyama et al. 2009](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jpts/21/2/21_2_195/_article)). A heels-up squat needs about 157 degrees of knee flexion ([Hemmerich et al. 2006](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=Hemmerich%20squatting%20kneeling%20range%20of%20motion%20activities%20daily%20living&resultType=core&format=json&pageSize=5)). Older adults' floor rises used 89–97 degrees of hip flexion, 118–129 of knee flexion and about 30 of ankle dorsiflexion ([Burton et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9958992/)). Roll-over risers used 25 degrees more knee flexion than those who rose from hands and feet, whom the authors suspect have "less knee mobility" ([Stutzenberger and Whited 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12452367/)). In squats, ankle range alone explained 45% of forward trunk lean, and all segment-length ratios together added only 15% ([Stronger by Science review](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/?p=53129)). Flexibility also tracked the sitting-rising score across 3,927 people aged 6–92 ([Brito et al. 2013][epmc-floor]).

Where the mass sits decides how the remaining paths are used. Obese adults rising from a chair kept the trunk more upright (a trunk angle of 73 against 49 degrees, read as measured from the horizontal, which fits the authors' "limiting trunk flexion"), pulled their feet back 50 mm against 5, and **loaded the knee twice as much (0.75 against 0.38 N·m/kg)** ([Sibella et al. 2003][epmc-chair]). Once tired, they leaned forward after all, to spare the knee ([Galli et al. 2000][epmc-chair]), and a higher body-mass index went with a faster trunk swing, which uses momentum ([Bollinger et al. 2019][epmc-chair]). Toddlers carry 25.5% of their mass in the head at 12 months and 21% at three years, against 5.8% in adult men ([crash-dummy design table](https://musculoskeletalkey.com/anthropomorphic-test-devices-and-injury-risk-assessments/)). They first rise through a bear stance or a squat, and only later, as balance and strength grow, through a half-kneel ([Starfish Therapies](https://starfishtherapies.com/2020/07/floor-to-stand)); a paediatric therapist demonstrates all three routes on video ([Dr. Lauren Baker](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hdQ8eey6Wm4&t=39s)). Children with achondroplasia, who have short limbs with a large head and trunk, work out wide-based paths that involve the hands. They push up from lying face down with the legs spread wide into sitting, kneel up on both knees, or roll face down and pull themselves up ([Beyond Achondroplasia](https://www.beyondachondroplasia.org/en/library/guidelines/123-lying-to-sitting-to-standing-up-in-achondroplasia)). Armour weighing 44% of body mass more than doubled the energy cost of walking (2.1–2.3 times) ([Askew et al. 2012](https://www.ndcn.ox.ac.uk/team/federico-formenti/publication_modal/374524)). A single-subject study found most joint ranges somewhat reduced in harness, while summaries of it report near-full range for everyday motions such as sitting down and standing up ([Jaquet et al. 2016](https://archive-ouverte.unige.ch/unige:85135)). A loaded body should therefore mostly keep its strategy and slow down, unless the load also locks joints, as the rigid ankle brace below did.

A deficit on one side makes the rise lopsided, not merely slow. Healthy young adults wearing one rigid ankle brace shifted toward asymmetrical patterns with more trunk rotation ([King and VanSant 1995][epmc-floor]). After a below-knee amputation, rises took 10.6 s on average; 55% of people needed a chair, and 55% led with the prosthetic limb into a half-kneel ([Klima et al. 2026][epmc-floor]). Tao's simulated character, with one elbow and the opposite knee locked, "relies on the remaining limbs to gain momentum" ([Tao et al. 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2205.00307)). A player body with one weaker or longer limb should pick a side and turn.

Size works against a body. If proportions and muscle quality stay the same, the moment demanded grows with mass times length, which is length to the fourth power. The moment available grows with muscle cross-section times moment arm, which is length cubed. So **a body twice as tall works twice as close to its limit** (a square-cube derivation, not a measurement). Large mammals answer by standing on straighter limbs, raising their limbs' mechanical advantage roughly with body mass to the power 0.25 ([Biewener 2005](https://cob.silverchair.com/jeb/article-split/208/9/1665/9381/Biomechanical-consequences-of-scaling)). Large four-legged animals show two solutions, set by body plan. Horses rise front first and throw the heavy head forward to unload the hindquarters ([EquiSearch](https://equisearch.com/articles/eqrolling3410/)). Cattle rise hind first onto their bent front knees ([WikiVet](https://en.wikivet.net/Special:MobileDiff/159419...159431)). Bodies that cannot muscle through still succeed by repetition. Cockroaches right themselves probabilistically, as leg flailing tips them over a lowered barrier ([Othayoth and Li 2021](https://elifesciences.org/articles/60233v1)). Domed turtles are righted by the shape of their shells ([Domokos and Várkonyi 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2562404)). Horses recovering from anaesthesia needed 4.5 attempts on average to reach the belly-down position without morphine, and 2.0 with it ([AGRIS record](https://agris.fao.org/search/fr/records/65dfed4663b8185d9cb0150f)). A varying number of attempts, with rests that grow with the shortfall in reserve, therefore has a basis in real bodies.

Timing follows capacity too, and the table below gives the anchors. Times from intermediate starts break the rise into stages. Older adults took 13.1 s from lying, 8.0 s from side-sitting and 3.9 s from half-kneeling before training, and 7.1, 4.6 and 1.5 s after it ([Seeley et al. 2026][epmc-floor]); practice and strength shorten the last, effortful stage most. **A slow rise is not the same motion slowed down.** Older adults' peak vertical velocity was 0.8 against 1.1 m/s and their peak vertical acceleration 3.5 against 4.7 m/s², with more turning and less direct trunk paths ([Schwickert et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5017442/)). No study measured pause lengths in human floor rising, so rests are best tied to the reserve needed for the next stage.

## Build a capacity-driven procedural rise, and keep learning for the corrections

The recommendation is a **capacity-driven procedural rise**. This is one controller that gets any body up by computing, from that body alone, which stances it can hold, how to move between them and how long each stage should take. It is tuned once, during development, over many sampled bodies. It keeps every rule the director has set: the body's own joints only, nothing lifting it, no clip played, and help allowed only while training ([October 22 decision](../../Correspondence/2026-10-22_NEVER_THE_BACKUP_ANIMATION.md); [October 30 message](../../Correspondence/2026-10-30_THE_GETTING_UP_FOR_THE_WHOLE_GAME.md)). Its run-time cost per unit is negligible. The same balance machinery that keeps a standing miner up becomes the machinery that raises it, so standing, stepping, falling and rising share one controller whose targets move. It is built in layers, and the first layer is needed whatever method is finally chosen.

Proportions and strength show through this design without any per-body code. They decide which stance families exist for this body, where its hands and feet land (in frames scaled by its own limb lengths), how long each supported phase lasts, how many supports it keeps, how its trunk and head counterbalance, which side leads, and how often it must try again. A strong, light, flexible body has every stance available and a small reserve, so it rises fast through a squat. A heavy, weak, stiff one loses the squat, keeps its hands down and rests. A toddler-proportioned one is pushed toward the bear route by its head share, and a body with one weak leg turns toward the other side. This is requirement (3) met by the same numbers that meet requirement (1). The director has already asked that a body's answers "be based on their capabilities" and that they never "switch from one kind of response to the other at a certain threshold" ([October 13](../../Correspondence/2026-10-13_HOW_A_PULLED_MINER_ANSWERS.md)). So the style settings should vary continuously with the reserve. Near the boundary between two stance families, the choice should follow the actual lying state and a stable preference, so that it does not flip with a tiny change of build.

The order of work follows the evidence. First mend the body, because every method inherits its faults: anatomical limits, clamped targets, human-scaled and speed-dependent strength, a working speed cap on light parts, and permanent per-frame checks. Measure that work across Small, Long and Round and at several frame rates until no frame shows a joint outside its range, a part over its speed bound, a NaN, or a drawn bone more than a few degrees from its physics part. Second, build a body sampler that draws bodies across the creator's intended range of proportions, masses, strengths and joint ranges, including the extremes. Change the bench's unit of evidence from "36 tries of Small" to success, time and frame faults over that population, reported by class of body. Third, write the body analysis and the strategy table, and check that their choices match the human ladder before any motion is judged: weak-and-heavy bodies should choose hands-and-feet routes, strong-and-light bodies a squat or half-kneel, toddler-like bodies the bear. Fourth, generalise the existing riser's targets into body-relative goals and its transitions into ramped, state-gated transfers. Fifth, tune the general settings over the sampled population. Jones's bar of at least 80% success over 16 disturbed runs is a reasonable first target inside the creator's range ([Jones 2011](https://users.cs.utah.edu/~benjones/ubc_2011_fall_jones_benjamin.pdf)). The director judges pictures for each body class against the references above. Only then time physics run headless for 1, 8, 16 and 32 copies of a body, to decide whether the learned corrector of layer 5 is affordable.

Several choices belong to the director, and nothing here promotes them from Open. The first is which attributes the creator exposes: the October 30 message leaves the list open, and joint range, limb symmetry and load all matter for getting up. The second is whether the creator's range should be bounded to bodies that can rise unaided. In two studies of elders in supported housing, 24% and 38% could not rise unaided ([Alexander et al. 1997][epmc-floor]; [Ulbrich et al. 2000][epmc-vansant]), so some builds may honestly be non-risers, and the game must decide whether that is allowed, prevented, or answered by another unit's help. The third is whether a small library of human stance families, written as body-relative goals rather than motions, counts as "pre-made" under the October 13 rule against "a pre-made animation" ([October 13](../../Correspondence/2026-10-13_HOW_A_PULLED_MINER_ANSWERS.md)). The fourth is whether a few seconds of optimisation per body design at creation would be acceptable, should per-design plans ever be added; the body analysis of layer 1 takes only milliseconds. The fifth is whether human motion capture from the commercial-safe CMU set may shape a learned corrector during training. The sixth is the target time to stand for each class, where human anchors run from about 4 s for an able adult to about 15 s for a very limited one.

The uncertainty is real, and it is stated here plainly. **No method of any family has a published success rate for getting up across a continuous space of body proportions and strengths.** The designed route's evidence is walking across proportions (Coros), rising in two dimensions (Jones), arbitrary bodies drawn kinematically (Spore), a few body edits carried out in physics (Lin and Huang), and robots. Its three-dimensional transition from hands and feet onto the feet has no published rate. A designed controller can also slide into the posed look the director rejected on October 9 if its gains are stiff and its targets fixed ([October 30 message](../../Correspondence/2026-10-30_THE_GETTING_UP_FOR_THE_WHOLE_GAME.md)). The defences in the sources are low gains made possible by gravity compensation, targets recomputed from state, unevenness between sides, and uneven timing ([Coros et al. 2010](http://www.cs.ubc.ca/~van/papers/2010-TOG-gbwc/paper.pdf); [How people get up](../2026-10-14_HowPeopleGetUp.md)). The reserve bands, the square-cube argument and the per-unit cost estimates are reasoning, not measurements. Cross-body learning is improving quickly: most of the cross-body results cited here appeared in 2025 and 2026. The verdict on learning should be revisited in six to twelve months, and sooner if the laptop turns out to have a GPU that MuJoCo Warp supports.

## Conclusion

The three requirements pull against each other for learned methods but line up for a capacity-driven one. A learned policy becomes general by learning to ignore differences between bodies, and it becomes natural by borrowing one captured body's style. Both forces flatten the individuality the director wants. Human science says a body's way up is a function of its capacity, so the numbers that make a controller general (masses, ranges, caps, the reserve) are the same numbers that make each body rise in its own way. The frame review also moved much of requirement (2) out of the controller and into the body and its drawing. No method can look natural on a body whose joints have no stops and whose boots can spin at 150 rad/s, and the cheapest gains in naturalness come before any change of method.

The practical shift is in what counts as evidence. "Any body" makes a sampled population of bodies the thing being measured. Success, time to stand and frame faults over that population, judged by class against real footage, replace the count of 36 tries of one miner. The human data also say that some builds should honestly fail to rise unaided, which turns a technical limit into a design decision about the creator's range. **For Wonder Gather, build the capacity-driven procedural rise now, starting with a body that cannot look broken. Tune it once over many sampled bodies, and add a single learned corrector only when a measured simulation speed makes one affordable.**
